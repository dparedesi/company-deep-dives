# Ethics, Controversies, and Risk Research Document

## Overview

This document compiles comprehensive research on Palantir Technologies' ethical controversies, regulatory risks, and business risks as of April 2026. The research draws from existing manuscript chapters (7 and 8), public disclosures, and documented corporate actions to provide specific, factual details on 15 critical topics spanning immigration enforcement, military AI, data privacy, governance concerns, and financial risks.

Palantir operates at the intersection of several high-stakes domains: national security, law enforcement, healthcare data, and military artificial intelligence. This positioning creates persistent ethical scrutiny and material business risks. The company's explicit "Western hard power" doctrine under CEO Alex Karp frames its work as a moral imperative to support Western democracies, but this ideological stance simultaneously creates reputational, regulatory, and competitive vulnerabilities.

Key themes emerging from this research include: (1) The depth and durability of Palantir's integration into US and allied government infrastructure, creating lock-in that protects revenue but amplifies failure consequences; (2) The evolution of ethical opposition from activist-NGO campaigns to mainstream institutional resistance involving doctors' unions, asset managers, and medical journals; (3) The acceleration of military AI adoption through Maven Smart System and NATO adoption, placing Palantir at the center of lethal autonomous weapons debates; (4) Financial governance concerns including substantial insider selling and executive compensation scrutiny; and (5) Regulatory headwinds from the EU AI Act that create compliance burdens and potential market exclusion risks.

## Detailed Findings

### ICE and ImmigrationOS Contract

Palantir's relationship with US Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) represents one of its most persistent and damaging controversies. The company has provided ICE with Investigative Case Management (ICM) software and other analytical tools for over a decade and a half. FOIA documents and investigative reporting in 2025 revealed the full depth of ICE's reliance on Palantir's systems. The contracts reportedly support immigrant identification, tracking, planning of workplace raids, and management of deportations, which critics connect to family separations and human rights concerns.

In 2024-2025, a new approximately $30 million contract for "ImmigrationOS" extended Palantir's reach beyond Homeland Security Investigations to wider ICE branches. This next-generation platform is designed to streamline immigrant identification and deportation processes across the entire agency. ImmigrationOS represents an escalation in the scope and integration of Palantir's ICE work, moving from specialized investigative support to agency-wide operational infrastructure.

Palantir CEO Alex Karp has consistently defended the ICE work, arguing that the company provides technology to legally constituted government agencies to carry out their lawful mandates. He emphasizes that it is preferable for a transparent Western company like Palantir, with internal ethical considerations and audit trails, to undertake such work rather than leaving governments to build more opaque systems. The company publicly states it has not contracted with ICE's Enforcement and Removal Operations or CBP in recent years, though critics counter that other ICE branches still support the same deportation pipeline.

Critics, including Amnesty International, ACLU, immigrant rights advocates, and some Palantir employees, argue that providing powerful analytical tools to ICE makes Palantir directly complicit in activities that result in human rights abuses. The efficiency and scale provided by Palantir's software, they contend, exacerbates the harm caused by controversial immigration policies. Amnesty International and investor coalitions such as the Investor Alliance for Human Rights now treat Palantir's ICE work as a flagship human rights concern and a material risk factor.

The ImmigrationOS contract has drawn criticism from prominent technology figures including Paul Graham, co-founder of Y Combinator. Graham's exact quote, shared on X (formerly Twitter), stated:

> "It's a very exciting time in tech right now. If you're a first-rate programmer, there are a huge number of other places you can go work rather than at the company building the infrastructure of the police state."

Ted Mabrey, Palantir's global head of commercial, responded that he's "looking forward to the next set of hires that decided to apply to Palantir after reading your post." Mabrey defended the work by referencing Operation Fallen Hero, the Department of Homeland Security's response to the murder of Agent Jaime Zapata by the Zetas. He stated: "When people are alive because of what you built, and others are dead because what you built was not yet good enough, you develop a very different perspective on the meaning of your work."

Graham pressed Palantir to "commit publicly on behalf of Palantir not to build things that help the government violate the US constitution," acknowledging such a commitment would have "no legal force" but hoping an employee would say "I didn't sign up for this" if asked to do something illegal.

Reporting has described ImmigrationOS as powering aspects of the US deportation crackdown, intensifying the debate beyond activist circles into mainstream tech discourse. The system provides "near real-time visibility" into self-deportations and helps ICE decide who to target for deportation operations.

### Predictive Policing and Racial Bias Concerns

Palantir's Gotham platform has been utilized by police departments including historically in New Orleans and Los Angeles for data analysis that includes elements of "predictive policing"—using historical crime data and other information to forecast where crimes are likely to occur or identify individuals perceived as being at higher risk of future criminal involvement.

Civil liberties advocates and AI ethics researchers raise grave concerns that predictive policing systems, often trained on biased historical crime data reflecting existing patterns of over-policing in marginalized communities, can perpetuate and amplify these biases. This can lead to disproportionate targeting, surveillance, and arrests, creating self-fulfilling prophecies. The lack of transparency in the algorithms and data used represents a major concern for due process and racial justice.

Palantir emphasizes that its tools are for organizing and analyzing data to support human investigators, not for making automated arrests or pre-crime determinations. The company highlights its "human-in-the-loop" philosophy as a safeguard against automated discrimination. However, critics argue that even human-supervised systems can embed and amplify historical biases if the underlying data reflects discriminatory policing patterns.

In 2024, Freedom of Information documents revealed that Palantir had discussions with the UK Ministry of Justice about using its analytics to calculate prisoners' risk of reoffending and to manage prison capacity. Civil liberties groups and some MPs raised concerns about delegating liberty-impacting risk assessments to corporate AI systems. Predictive risk scoring in criminal justice is increasingly seen as a constitutional and human rights issue in contemporary legal scholarship.

The controversy extends beyond street-level policing to sentencing, parole, and prison management. The fundamental concern is that data-driven predictions about future behavior, even when probabilistic rather than deterministic, can shift criminal justice focus from evidence of actual wrongdoing to statistical risk profiles, potentially undermining the presumption of innocence and rights to due process.

### Israel and Gaza Military Support

Palantir has openly supported Israel's military and security apparatus, providing data analytics and AI-assisted surveillance and targeting systems. This support has drawn scrutiny from NGOs and institutional investors concerned that these technologies may contribute to violations of international humanitarian law in occupied Palestinian territories. The intensity of these concerns grew significantly during the 2023-24 Gaza conflict and its aftermath.

Palantir frames Israel as a key Western ally and democracy facing existential security threats. The company argues that its tools increase precision and reduce collateral damage by enabling more accurate intelligence analysis and targeting. This positioning aligns with Palantir's broader mission of supporting Western democracies and their allies. CEO Alex Karp has been explicit about viewing support for Israel's security as consistent with Palantir's "Western hard power" doctrine.

Human rights organizations and some investors counter that powerful targeting and predictive tools can also scale harm, and that there is little external transparency into how they are actually used in conflict zones. Regardless of stated intent, providing advanced AI systems to militaries engaged in contested operations raises questions about complicity in potential war crimes or disproportionate use of force.

In 2024, Storebrand Asset Management, a major Norwegian investor with approximately $100 billion in assets under management, divested from Palantir explicitly citing human rights concerns related to its work in Israel and Gaza. This decision signals that the controversy has moved beyond activist circles into mainstream institutional investment. Storebrand's exclusion statement specifically referenced concerns that Palantir's technology could be used to violate international humanitarian law in Gaza.

The issue deepens reputational risk in Europe, fuels BDS-adjacent campaigns, and creates potential future legal exposure if courts or tribunals judge certain operations illegal. The Israel/Gaza controversy has become material to Palantir's European growth prospects, as institutional investors with ESG mandates and European government clients with political sensitivities may avoid or terminate contracts due to perceived association with controversial military operations.

### NHS Federated Data Platform Controversy

Palantir has secured significant contracts with NHS England to provide a "Federated Data Platform" (FDP) using Foundry, aimed at improving patient care, managing waiting lists, and optimizing hospital operations. This has sparked concern among privacy advocates and medical professionals regarding a private US tech company gaining deep access to sensitive national patient data.

With FDP in rollout phase across NHS trusts, the controversy has shifted from prospective concerns to concrete institutional opposition. In 2024, the British Medical Journal published an editorial titled "NHS England must cancel its contract with Palantir" arguing that keeping the partnership risks eroding public trust in health data and recommended cancellation. The BMJ, one of the world's oldest and most respected medical journals, taking this position represents a significant escalation of professional medical opposition.

In 2025, the British Medical Association's Annual Representative Meeting passed a motion declaring Palantir an unacceptable partner for the FDP and instructed the union to lobby against its use and support clinicians who refuse to use the system. The BMA is the trade union representing doctors in the UK, with over 150,000 members. Its formal opposition signals that resistance to Palantir's NHS role has become institutionalized within the medical profession.

Palantir and NHS England assert that the platform helps improve healthcare efficiency and patient outcomes, with robust data security and compliance with UK health data regulations. The "federated" aspect is intended to allow different NHS trusts to connect their data while maintaining local control. Palantir states it does not own or monetize NHS data. NHS England's benefits dashboard reports hundreds of thousands of patients removed from waiting lists and reduced administrative delays.

Campaign groups like No Palantir in the NHS and Corporate Watch have used FOI requests to argue that FDP rollout has been patchy, with questions about usability, uptake, and staff burden at some trusts. A tension exists between NHS England's stated benefits and FOI-based narratives about low adoption and usability issues. The same system that de-duplicates waiting lists and improves care coordination can also be perceived as a "national health surveillance backbone," and trust in public health institutions may be damaged if patients perceive their data is managed by opaque foreign tech vendors.

Public consent for large-scale health data use is a precondition for digitally empowered health systems, and controversial vendors can threaten that consent. The NHS FDP controversy illustrates how ethical opposition can progress from activist campaigns to mainstream professional institutions, creating material risks to contract renewal and expansion.

### GDPR Investigations and Privacy Concerns

In Europe, Palantir's data processing practices face growing scrutiny under GDPR. Dutch privacy advocacy group SOMI (Stichting Privacy First) and other European privacy advocates have called for EU-wide investigations into how Palantir processes Europeans' data and whether its deployments comply with GDPR requirements, including exploring potential collective actions.

Palantir maintains that its platforms are designed to help clients comply with their own data protection obligations and that its technical safeguards, access controls, and audit trails meet or exceed regulatory requirements. The company highlights built-in features including granular access controls, data minimization tools, purpose specification, and audit trails designed to protect privacy and ensure accountability.

Privacy groups question whether these claimed access controls and audit trails are sufficient to ensure GDPR compliance in practice, particularly when deployed by government agencies whose oversight may be limited. The emergence of GDPR-focused apps that help individuals file data access and deletion requests targeting companies like Palantir signals growing bottom-up pressure from European citizens.

This European-specific controversy provides a concrete anchor for broader privacy concerns and connects directly to regulatory risk. The "mosaic effect," where combining disparate, non-sensitive data points can reveal highly sensitive personal details, represents a significant risk inherent to Palantir's core technology. The sheer power to aggregate and analyze data creates a high potential for abuse, especially if oversight is lax or policies are weak.

The risk of "function creep"—data collected for one purpose being used for another, unintended purpose—is ever-present. Once powerful data integration platforms are deployed, the temptation to expand their scope to new uses can be significant, particularly within government agencies where oversight may be limited.

### EU AI Act Impact and Compliance

The EU AI Act entered into force in August 2024, creating a new regulatory regime for AI systems in Europe. Many Palantir deployments—particularly in law enforcement, border control, critical infrastructure, and healthcare—fall squarely under the Act's "high risk" category. The Act represents the world's first comprehensive AI law and sets a precedent that other jurisdictions may follow.

The Act's implementation timeline is phased: prohibitions and AI literacy obligations apply from February 2025, general purpose AI and governance rules from August 2025, and high-risk system obligations phasing in through 2027. This creates risk that Palantir or its European clients may fall foul of documentation, transparency, or human oversight requirements. High-risk AI systems must undergo conformity assessment, be registered in an EU database, meet strict data governance requirements, and provide transparency to users.

There is also a strategic tension between Palantir's "Operational Sovereignty" promise—giving institutions full control of their AI—and a European regulatory regime that constrains what sovereign use of AI can legally look like. The EU AI Act's high-risk categories require strict compliance and registration in EU databases, creating both compliance burdens and potential market exclusion risks.

This sits at the intersection of market, geopolitical, and regulatory risk. As EU and other states push for "sovereign AI" infrastructure that is not overly dependent on US vendors, Palantir faces the prospect of reduced addressable market in sovereignty-focused regions, increased compliance costs, and potential exclusion from certain government and regulated-sector contracts.

Palantir's public policy submissions have argued for "balanced" AI regulation that promotes innovation while protecting rights, but the EU AI Act's prescriptive approach to high-risk systems may constrain aspects of the company's business model. The Act's emphasis on transparency and explainability may be difficult to reconcile with some national security and law enforcement use cases where operational security is paramount.

### Military AI Ethics, Project Maven, and Lethal Autonomous Weapons

Palantir has been involved in US Department of Defense initiatives like Project Maven, which uses AI to analyze drone surveillance footage. While Palantir states its role is focused on data processing and augmenting human analysts, not on developing autonomous weapons that make "kill decisions," its work in military AI contributes to broader societal anxieties about an AI arms race and the potential for lethal autonomous weapon systems (LAWS).

In late 2023, Palantir won a roughly $480 million contract to build and operate the Maven Smart System for the Department of Defense. NATO subsequently agreed a multibillion dollar deal to acquire Maven Smart System for alliance-wide use, consolidating Palantir's role in coalition warfighting infrastructure. In 2025, the US Army signed a long-term enterprise agreement with a ceiling of up to $10 billion, consolidating over 70 existing contracts and making Palantir a central AI and data platform across the service.

The Maven Smart System and its NATO deployment use large language models and generative AI to support battle management, planning, and targeting recommendations, which intensifies ethical concerns even if humans still sign off on lethal actions. The designation of Maven Smart System as a formal Pentagon program of record in 2026 further elevates the stakes: Maven is no longer an experiment but an established, budgeted programme.

Alex Karp is explicitly pro-military regarding Western democracies, believing it is Palantir's duty to provide them with the best technology for defense. He has criticized other tech companies for shying away from such work. Palantir emphasizes that humans always make the ultimate lethal decisions and that their AI is meant to assist, not replace, human judgment.

Opponents fear that even AI for analysis and targeting support can lower the threshold for the use of force and contribute to an "algorithmic kill chain," potentially leading to a slippery slope towards lethal autonomous weapon systems. The "black box" nature of some AI can obscure accountability for targeting decisions. UN and G7 debates about lethal autonomous weapons highlight the risk that AI lowers the threshold for conflict.

Pope Francis's 2024 address to the G7 argued that machines must never decide life and death, signalling that the LAWS debate has moved into mainstream diplomacy. The Bletchley Declaration on AI Safety, signed by 28 countries including the US, UK, and China in 2023, highlighted these frontier AI risks, and Palantir's growing role in high-stakes agentic deployments places it at the centre of this emerging concern.

Palantir is no longer a niche defense supplier but a central actor in Western AI warfighting infrastructure. This deepens alignment with Karp's "Western hard power" doctrine and increases the stakes of any failure, accident, or scandal. The company is firmly positioned among tech companies willing to engage in controversial military AI work, solidifying its defense ties but potentially alienating segments of the public and some tech talent.

### Internal Dissent and Employee Protests

The ICE and military contracts have generated internal dissent at Palantir. Human rights organizations, immigrant rights advocates, and even some former and current Palantir employees have argued that by providing powerful analytical tools to ICE, Palantir is directly complicit in activities that result in human rights abuses. The Paul Graham quote about first-rate programmers having "a huge number of other places you can go work" captures the recruitment challenge.

Ethical controversies can hinder recruitment and retention of top AI engineers who may prefer employers perceived as less politically entangled, echoing the Google Project Maven episode where employee protests led the company to withdraw from certain military AI work. The fact that Palantir's ethical opposition comes from mainstream technology figures like Paul Graham suggests recruiting challenges among engineers who may avoid employers associated with controversial government work.

Palantir's strong ideological framing—the "Western hard power" narrative articulated in CEO Alex Karp's February 2025 book "The Technological Republic"—may itself alienate potential employees who prefer vendor neutrality or who object to working on military and immigration enforcement projects. The company has had to navigate the tension between its explicit ideological positioning and the need to attract top technical talent in a competitive market where many peers avoid controversial government work.

The company maintains that its work supports Western democracies and lawful government mandates, and that employees who join understand and support this mission. However, the scale and persistence of internal dissent, including resignations over specific contracts, suggests that not all employees fully align with the company's ideological stance.

### Insider Selling and Governance Concerns

Significant insider selling has occurred at Palantir. Peter Thiel, co-founder, has sold approximately $570 million in shares. Other insiders including CEO Alex Karp, Stephen Cohen (co-founder), Shyam Sankar (CTO), Ryan Taylor, and Matthew Glazer have also sold substantial amounts.

While such selling can reflect normal diversification and pre-planned trading programs, the scale and breadth at a premium valuation has drawn investor attention. Insider selling at this magnitude can send negative signals to the market about insider confidence, contribute to valuation pressure, and create governance risk if shareholders perceive insufficient alignment with long-term value creation.

CEO Alex Karp's corporate jet expenses of approximately $17.2 million have also attracted scrutiny, adding to governance concerns around leadership concentration and corporate spending. This level of executive perk spending is notable even among large-cap technology companies and contributes to perceptions of an unconventional corporate culture.

The combination of substantial insider selling and significant executive perks can contribute to perceptions that management is not fully aligned with shareholder interests. The analyst community has become polarized on governance and valuation: bulls cite a "Category of One" positioning and an "ontology moat that proves itself," while bears maintain that the valuation leaves no room for missteps.

This disagreement itself is a risk factor, increasing stock volatility and the potential for sharp sentiment shifts. Governance concerns can affect investor appetite, particularly among institutional investors with strict ESG criteria or governance screens. The breadth of insider selling across multiple executives, rather than being concentrated in one or two individuals, amplifies these concerns.

### Valuation Risk and Market Sentiment

Palantir's stock often trades at a premium valuation compared to many peers, predicated on expectations of sustained high growth and AI leadership. Investor commentary increasingly flags Palantir's valuation as "too high" relative to its revenue scale and independence, especially versus bundled cloud players.

In early 2026, the stock declined approximately 26% from an all-time high of around $207.52 amid broader AI disruption fears across the software sector, illustrating the execution risk embedded in the premium valuation. The stock's volatility reflects the high expectations priced into shares and the sensitivity to any indication that growth may be slowing.

Q3 2025 showed revenue growth of approximately 63% year over year and US commercial revenue up more than 120% year over year, with raised guidance to roughly $4.4 billion for 2025. Expectations are now anchored to extremely high commercial growth, so any slowdown in AIP adoption would have outsized market impact.

The analyst community has become polarized: bulls cite a "Category of One" positioning and an "ontology moat that proves itself," while bears maintain that the valuation leaves no room for missteps. This disagreement creates volatility and the potential for sharp sentiment shifts depending on quarterly results and guidance.

Valuation risk is compounded by the company's high exposure to government spending cycles and the political sensitivity of some contracts. Any indication that ethical controversies are affecting commercial growth or government contract renewals could trigger significant multiple compression. The stock's premium valuation assumes continued execution on both growth and profitability fronts, with little margin for error.

### Technology and Competitive Risk

The field of artificial intelligence, particularly machine learning and Large Language Models (LLMs), is advancing at an unprecedented rate. New architectures, more efficient algorithms, or fundamentally different AI paradigms could emerge, potentially rendering aspects of Palantir's current AI stack less competitive or obsolete if the company cannot adapt with sufficient speed.

Palantir's platforms (Gotham, Foundry, Apollo, AIP) are immensely complex software systems. As they evolve with new features and integrations, maintaining stability, performance, security, and backward compatibility across diverse and numerous deployment environments becomes an increasing engineering challenge. Accumulation of technical debt could hinder future innovation or lead to reliability issues.

The company is increasingly an orchestration and control layer atop a shifting landscape of foundation models from providers such as OpenAI, Anthropic, and open-source communities. Model performance, licensing terms, or safety constraints can change outside Palantir's control. Supply constraints or pricing changes for leading model providers could increase costs or reduce differentiation.

Competition comes from multiple directions: Hyperscale cloud providers (AWS, Azure, GCP) continuously enhance their native data, analytics, and AI services, offering integrated solutions at scale, often at lower price points or bundled with infrastructure. Data platform specialists (Snowflake, Databricks) offer strong alternatives for data warehousing, data lakehouses, and data science/ML workloads. Both are building full AI platforms that compete directly with AIP.

Market demand might shift towards more modular, simpler, or open-source solutions. "Good enough" solutions from competitors at a lower price point could erode Palantir's appeal for less complex use cases. The "build vs. buy" decision could increasingly favor "build" for certain organizations as AI tools become more accessible.

### Agentic AI Safety and Operational Risk

AIP and Maven Smart System explicitly place LLMs in operational workflows where agents can call tools, change workflows, and generate actions at scale. This increases the risk of unintended consequences if prompts, policies, or guardrails fail. In defence or critical infrastructure contexts, misconfigured agents could route supplies, set priorities, or influence target recommendations in ways that are difficult to audit or reverse.

The Bletchley Declaration on AI Safety, signed by major nations in 2023, highlighted these frontier AI risks, and Palantir's growing role in high-stakes agentic deployments places it at the centre of this emerging concern. An error, data bias, or prompt manipulation in an agentic system can propagate into real operational actions before humans can intervene.

Given the highly sensitive nature of the data Palantir's platforms process and manage (national security, critical infrastructure, corporate proprietary data), they represent an extremely high-value target for sophisticated state-sponsored cyber actors, industrial espionage, and organized cybercriminals. A significant security breach of Palantir's own systems or a major client environment through a vulnerability in Palantir's software would be catastrophic.

Internal assessments within the US Army have flagged next-generation command and control platforms involving Palantir and partners as "very high risk" from a security standpoint, underscoring institutional concern about cyber and system vulnerabilities in battlefield networks. Palantir's 10-K states explicitly that a material security breach could trigger regulatory penalties and mass client loss.

### Key Person and Leadership Risk

Palantir relies heavily on the vision and leadership of CEO Alex Karp. His departure could create uncertainty. Karp is the primary architect and articulator of Palantir's "Western hard power" doctrine, and his 2025 book "The Technological Republic" frames the company's mission as defending a fragile Western order that lacks ambition. This ideological framing is unusual among major technology companies and creates both differentiation and risk.

The loss of other key executives or a significant number of highly skilled engineers and data scientists could disrupt innovation, client relationships, and operational execution. The broader AI talent war, where big tech spends hundreds of billions on AI and pays aggressive compensation, increases the opportunity cost for Palantir engineers and intensifies retention challenges.

Key-person risk is compounded by the concentration of insider selling among multiple executives. When significant shareholders including Thiel, Karp, Cohen, Sankar, Taylor, and Glazer have all sold substantial amounts, it creates questions about long-term alignment and retention. The company's unusual governance structure, with dual-class shares that give insiders disproportionate voting control, amplifies these concerns.

Palantir's unconventional corporate culture, including significant executive perks like the $17.2 million in jet expenses for CEO Karp, may create cultural challenges in integrating acquired companies or attracting talent from more traditional technology companies. The strong ideological framing may alienate employees or partners who prefer a more neutral corporate stance.
