## The questions that remain

Every deep-dive analysis of a company at Palantir's stage of evolution ends with more questions than answers, and this one is no exception. The most consequential unknown is the durability of AIP's competitive position. The platform's early success has been remarkable, driving triple-digit commercial revenue growth and converting boot camp attendees into paying customers at impressive rates. But AIP is still young enough that its long-term stickiness remains unproven. Palantir does not disclose detailed cohort retention data, making it difficult to assess whether customers who adopted AIP in 2023 and 2024 are expanding their deployments, maintaining steady usage, or quietly evaluating alternatives. Net revenue retention figures are encouraging, but they measure revenue growth from existing customers, not the depth of operational integration that determines whether a customer would leave if a cheaper alternative appeared. The next two years of quarterly results will reveal whether AIP creates the kind of compounding lock-in that Palantir's ontology-driven approach promises, or whether enterprises treat it as a useful but ultimately replaceable layer in their AI stack.

The international question remains equally open. Palantir's commercial success is overwhelmingly concentrated in the United States, where the company's defense relationships, cultural alignment, and language advantage create a favorable operating environment. European markets present a paradox: they represent enormous potential demand for operational AI, particularly as the EU AI Act creates compliance requirements that Palantir's governance capabilities could address, but political sensitivity around American defense contractors accessing European public sector data constrains adoption. Japan, South Korea, and Australia offer more promising terrain given shared security concerns with the United States, but these markets require substantial local investment and relationship building that scales more slowly than domestic expansion. The trajectory of international commercial revenue over the next several years will determine whether Palantir becomes a global platform or remains predominantly an American phenomenon with a defense-centric international footprint.

The governance question deserves more attention than it typically receives. Palantir's dual-class share structure concentrates voting power among founders and early investors, Peter Thiel, Alex Karp, and Stephen Cohen maintained Class F shares with 49.99% voting interest regardless of their economic ownership. This structure provides operational stability and protects against activist investors, but it also means that ordinary shareholders have essentially no influence over corporate decisions. Insider selling has been substantial: Thiel sold approximately $570 million in shares, Karp's compensation of $6.8 billion in 2024 raised governance concerns, and other executives including Stephen Cohen, Shyam Sankar, Ryan Taylor, and Andrew Glazer have also sold significant positions. While insider selling is common at high-growth technology companies and does not necessarily signal lack of confidence, the scale and velocity of selling at Palantir warrants monitoring. The tension between a governance structure designed for control and a public market that increasingly demands accountability will shape Palantir's evolution as it matures from a high-growth disruptor into an established enterprise software company.

The broader question is whether the Western alliance will ultimately build its AI infrastructure on a foundation of democratic accountability or whether the urgency of competing with China and Russia will prioritize capability over governance. Palantir sits at the center of this tension, building the most powerful operational AI platforms in the world while insisting that its commitment to Western values provides a moral framework that authoritarian alternatives lack. Whether that framework proves durable under the pressure of real-world deployment, where the line between legitimate surveillance and civil liberties erosion is perpetually contested, will determine not just Palantir's commercial success but its place in the historical narrative of how democratic societies adapted artificial intelligence to the demands of national security. The company's trajectory over the next five years will provide answers that extend far beyond quarterly earnings reports and stock price movements.
