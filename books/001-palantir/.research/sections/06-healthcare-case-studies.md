## Healthcare: from operating rooms to national systems

Palantir's expansion into healthcare represents one of the most significant extensions of its technology beyond its defense and intelligence origins. The company has moved from pandemic-response work to enduring deployments that span individual hospital systems and entire national health services. What makes healthcare particularly compelling as a use case is that, unlike classified defense work, the results are increasingly quantifiable and public. Hospitals and health systems publish outcome metrics, enabling a clearer assessment of what Palantir's platforms actually deliver.

The healthcare deployments also illustrate how Palantir's architecture scales from a single institution to a nationwide system. Tampa General Hospital in Florida operates as a commercial Foundry and AIP deployment within one academic medical center, while the NHS Federated Data Platform in England spans hundreds of hospital trusts and integrated care boards. Both rely on the same foundational approach: an ontology that models the entities and relationships of healthcare delivery, topped with agents that propose or take actions. The contrast between these two deployments, one hospital-level and one system-level, reveals how Palantir's technology adapts to different scales of healthcare operations.

Tampa General Hospital has emerged as the best-documented commercial healthcare case study for Palantir. The academic medical center created a "Care Coordination Center" called CareComm using Foundry and AIP to optimize patient flow, manage staffing levels, reduce emergency department wait times, and improve overall hospital operational efficiency. The results, published by TGH and recognized through industry awards, provide concrete numbers that are rare in the world of enterprise software deployments. The hospital achieved an 83 percent reduction in patient placement time, meaning patients spend dramatically less time waiting to be assigned to appropriate care units after admission decisions are made. This represents a meaningful operational improvement in a setting where emergency department overcrowding and admission delays are persistent problems.

The Tampa General deployment also produced a 28 percent decrease in post-anesthesia care unit holds. PACU holds occur when patients cannot move from recovery to their inpatient beds because bed assignments or cleaning workflows are delayed. A reduction of this magnitude translates to faster throughput in surgical suites and better utilization of expensive operating room time. Perhaps most clinically significant is the 30 percent reduction in sepsis length of stay. Sepsis is a life-threatening condition that requires rapid, coordinated care. Shortening length of stay for sepsis patients suggests that earlier identification, faster treatment initiation, or more efficient discharge planning are improving outcomes. TGH leadership has cited over $12 million in annual savings from these operational improvements, though the specific components of that savings figure include reduced labor costs, better capacity utilization, and avoided penalties or readmissions.

The technology behind these results involves deep integration with Tampa General's existing electronic health record system, which is Epic. Foundry does not replace Epic but rather sits atop it, pulling data from the EHR and other hospital systems into a unified ontology. The ontology models patients, beds, staff, clinical units, equipment, and the relationships between them. When a patient is admitted, the system knows not just that a bed exists somewhere, but which specific bed with which capabilities is appropriate for that patient's clinical needs, which staff are assigned, and what cleaning or preparation steps must occur first. This granular modeling enables agents to propose specific actions rather than just displaying data.

The CareComm system exemplifies agentic workflows in healthcare rather than passive dashboards. Rather than presenting data and leaving humans to interpret and act, AIP agents recommend bed assignments, propose staffing changes based on predicted patient volume, and escalate at-risk patients before they deteriorate. The 83 percent reduction in placement time likely reflects agents pre-computing optimal bed assignments as admission decisions are being made, rather than staff manually searching for availability after the fact. Similarly, reduced PACU holds suggest that discharge and bed cleaning workflows are being triggered earlier in the surgical process, with agents coordinating handoffs between units. TGH won the Joint Commission's 2025 Healthcare Innovation Award partly for this work, validating the deployment within the mainstream healthcare quality community.

While Tampa General operates at the hospital level, Palantir's work with England's National Health Service operates at the scale of an entire national health system. The NHS Federated Data Platform contract, valued at approximately 480 million pounds over seven years, represents Palantir's largest and most controversial healthcare deployment. Palantir serves as the core technology provider, though the platform operates under NHS England governance. By late September 2025, 77 NHS hospital trusts were live and reporting benefits, with 150 trusts having signed memoranda of understanding to join, plus all 41 integrated care boards. This scale makes the FDP deployment substantially larger than a typical hospital IT project and more comparable to a national infrastructure program.

The FDP has delivered quantified system-level outcomes across four major solution areas that NHS England publishes on a public benefits dashboard. The Inpatient Care Coordination Solution has enabled approximately 80,923 additional patients to be treated in theatres through better scheduling and waiting list management. This figure represents surgical procedures that happened because scheduling algorithms, working on integrated data across theatres and specialties, identified capacity that would otherwise have gone unused. A further 77,206 patients have been safely removed from inpatient waiting lists where treatment was no longer clinically needed, indicating that the platform is helping validate and clean lists rather than just managing them.

The Outpatients Care Coordination Solution has facilitated 213,101 patients being safely requested for removal from outpatient waiting lists. In a system where waiting lists for specialist consultations can stretch for months, removing patients who no longer need appointments or have received care elsewhere creates capacity for those still waiting. The Referral to Treatment validation tool has reviewed over 2.2 million records, resulting in 563,239 people being removed from waiting lists where care was no longer required. This represents a systematic data quality and validation exercise at national scale, something that would be extraordinarily difficult without a unified data platform.

The OPTICA discharge product has achieved a 14 to 15 percent reduction in delayed discharge days for long-stay patients, with more than 206,000 patients discharged using OPTICA-supported workflows. Delayed discharges occur when patients are clinically ready to leave the hospital but cannot be discharged because of downstream bottlenecks such as lack of home care support, rehabilitation bed availability, or social services assessment. A 15 percent reduction in delayed discharge days for long-stay patients represents substantial capacity liberation within hospitals, as each bed freed from a medically unnecessary stay can accommodate a patient from the emergency department or waiting list.

These figures illustrate "operational sovereignty" at health-system scale rather than hospital-level improvements alone. The shared ontology enables multiple micro-workflows to compound: theatre scheduling, waiting list validation, discharge coordination, and bed management all operate on the same data foundation. When a patient is discharged using OPTICA, that bed becomes immediately visible to theatre scheduling algorithms. When a referral is validated as unnecessary through the RTT tool, capacity is freed for other patients. The compounding effect of many micro-workflows operating on shared data is part of what Palantir means by operational sovereignty, and the NHS metrics provide concrete evidence of this effect at scale.

However, the NHS FDP has generated substantial opposition that illustrates the political and ethical dimensions of healthcare data platforms. The British Medical Association, representing over 150,000 doctors in the UK, voted in 2025 to oppose the rollout and lobby for termination of existing Palantir contracts. The BMA motion cited privacy, governance, and trust concerns, arguing that a private US technology company should not have deep access to sensitive national patient data. A 2024 BMJ editorial titled "NHS England must cancel its contract with Palantir" exemplifies how even measurable operational gains are being weighed against perceived sovereignty and ethical risks. The controversy centers not just on what Palantir's software does, but on who controls it and what precedents it sets for public-private partnerships in critical infrastructure.

The technical reality of what the FDP does is relatively straightforward: it connects data from NHS trusts that have historically operated as separate fiefdoms with incompatible systems. Each trust runs its own patient administration systems, scheduling software, and clinical applications. The FDP creates a federated layer where data can be queried and analyzed across trusts without requiring every trust to standardize on the same underlying systems. This "federated" approach means that data stays locally controlled at the trust level, but the platform provides unified analytics and workflow capabilities. The ontology models patients, pathways, appointments, clinical resources, and administrative status across the entire English healthcare system rather than within any single institution.

Despite the impressive macro-level metrics, Freedom of Information requests and campaign groups such as "No Palantir in the NHS" have collected feedback from staff describing issues including limited adoption, poor usability, restricted data access, technical barriers for analysts, and significant staff time spent wrestling with the system. This illustrates that even successful deployments can experience substantial front-line friction that threatens long-term stickiness. The same platform that de-duplicates waiting lists and improves care coordination can be perceived by front-line staff as difficult to use or restricting access to data they previously had through other means. The tension between NHS England's stated benefits and FOI-based narratives about low adoption and usability issues highlights that macro-level success metrics do not guarantee universal front-line acceptance.

Beyond Tampa General and the NHS, Palantir has made inroads into other segments of the healthcare ecosystem. In pharmaceuticals, Sanofi uses Foundry to accelerate R&D processes, optimize clinical trials, improve manufacturing quality and yield, and enhance supply chain visibility for pharmaceuticals. The value proposition for pharma companies is accelerating time-to-market for new drugs and optimizing global operations across research, manufacturing, and distribution. While Sanofi has not published specific ROI figures for its Palantir deployment, the pharmaceutical industry faces intense pressure to shorten development timelines and improve manufacturing efficiency, making data platforms that can connect research, clinical, and manufacturing operations strategically valuable.

A major healthcare payer signed a five-year, $26 million deal within five weeks of an AIP trial, illustrating how compressed sales cycles and rapid trial-to-deal conversion are reshaping the ROI narrative in healthcare insurance. Insurance companies face complex underwriting decisions, claims processing workflows, and fraud detection challenges that benefit from the kind of data integration and agentic automation that AIP provides. The fact that a payer went from trial to multimillion-dollar commitment in five weeks suggests that the boot camp approach, which has proven effective in other commercial verticals, works equally well in healthcare administration. This rapid conversion contrasts sharply with traditional healthcare IT sales cycles that can stretch for months or years.

The healthcare payer use case typically involves integrating data from claims, medical records, provider networks, and member information to support underwriting, claims adjudication, fraud detection, and member outreach. Agents can flag claims for review, propose prior authorization decisions, identify members who would benefit from care management outreach, or detect potential fraud patterns. The ROI for payers comes from reduced medical costs through better care management, avoided fraudulent payments, faster claims processing, and improved risk adjustment accuracy. A five-year, $26 million contract represents meaningful annual revenue for Palantir and suggests substantial operational budget for the payer to invest in the platform.

COVID-19 pandemic response work provided the initial entry point for Palantir into healthcare at scale. During the pandemic, Palantir's platforms were used by various government health agencies globally including the US Department of Health and Human Services and the UK's National Health Service. Applications included integrating data on infection rates, hospital capacity, testing, and patient outcomes; managing the supply chain for critical medical equipment such as ventilators and PPE; supporting vaccine distribution logistics; and forecasting disease spread. These pandemic-era deployments evolved into longer-term commercial and public sector relationships. The NHS FDP grew directly out of COVID-era work, and Tampa General's relationship with Palantir similarly accelerated during the pandemic when rapid data integration became a matter of urgent operational necessity.

The pandemic work also demonstrated Palantir's ability to stand up deployments quickly when urgency overrides typical procurement and implementation processes. What took weeks or months during emergency conditions later provided proof points for more sustained deployments. The contrast between emergency pandemic work and routine healthcare operations is significant: pandemic work was about situational awareness and rapid response, while ongoing healthcare operations require sustained efficiency improvements, clinical workflow integration, and regulatory compliance. Palantir's ability to transition from emergency response to sustained operations at places like Tampa General and within the NHS indicates that the technology can adapt across these different modes of healthcare delivery.

The healthcare deployments also illustrate how Palantir's "Western hard power" doctrine, which primarily frames its defense work, translates to healthcare. The company positions its technology as essential infrastructure for Western institutions facing complex operational challenges. In healthcare, this means arguing that fragmented, inefficient health systems in Western democracies need better data integration to compete with system-level challenges like aging populations, rising costs, and post-pandemic backlogs. The framing shifts from national security to health system resilience, but the underlying thesis that institutions need powerful, sovereign data platforms remains consistent.

What distinguishes healthcare from Palantir's traditional government work is that the outcomes are largely unclassified and publicly discussable. Tampa General can publish exact percentage reductions in length of stay. NHS England can publish exact counts of additional patients treated and records validated. This transparency creates a different kind of evidence base for Palantir's value proposition than the classified work that built its reputation. The healthcare case studies provide concrete, auditable metrics that potential customers in other industries can evaluate without taking claims on faith. This may be one reason healthcare has become a more prominent part of Palantir's customer storytelling in recent years.

The healthcare ROI story also differs from defense or intelligence in that benefits accrue directly to patients and staff rather than primarily to institutional mission effectiveness. An 83 percent reduction in patient placement time means patients spend less time waiting in uncomfortable emergency department hallways. A 30 percent reduction in sepsis length of stay means faster recovery and potentially better outcomes. Tens of thousands of additional surgical procedures in the NHS mean shorter waiting lists for people in pain or declining health. This human dimension to the metrics makes healthcare deployments particularly powerful as illustrations of Palantir's impact, even as they raise higher stakes for ethical questions about who controls sensitive health data.

Looking forward, healthcare represents both an opportunity and a risk for Palantir. The opportunity lies in the fact that healthcare systems globally face mounting operational pressures: aging populations, workforce shortages, rising costs, and post-pandemic backlogs. Data platforms that can improve efficiency without requiring massive capital investment in new facilities or equipment are strategically valuable. The risk lies in the fact that healthcare data is among the most sensitive data that exists, and public trust is fragile. The NHS controversy demonstrates that operational gains alone may not sustain a deployment if perceived sovereignty, privacy, or trust issues create sustained institutional opposition. Tampa General's success suggests that when hospital leadership, clinicians, and technology align, measurable improvements are possible. The NHS experience suggests that scaling that success to a national level introduces political, ethical, and cultural complexity that technology alone cannot resolve.

The contrast between Tampa General and the NHS illustrates a broader pattern in Palantir's healthcare work. At the institutional level, where a single hospital or health system makes its own procurement and implementation decisions, deployments can move quickly and demonstrate results. At the system level, where multiple stakeholders, professional associations, and public opinion all have a voice, the same technology becomes subject to debates about data sovereignty, public trust, and the role of private companies in critical infrastructure. Both levels are relevant to understanding Palantir's healthcare opportunity, and the tension between them will shape how healthcare customers evaluate the ROI of partnering with Palantir.
