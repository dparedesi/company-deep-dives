## Commercial deployments at scale

Palantir's commercial business has evolved dramatically since the introduction of AIP in 2023, with US commercial customer count growing from 143 at the end of 2022 to 262 by Q1 2024 and revenue growth exceeding 120 percent year over year by Q3 2025. The boot camp model, free intensive workshops where customer teams build working agents on their own data in days rather than months, has fundamentally changed the go-to-market approach. Palantir ran an estimated 660 AIP boot camps in Q1 2024 alone, up from around 324 in Q4 2023, with conversion to paying customers occurring in 5 to 10 percent of cases within 3 to 6 months. This acceleration in commercial adoption has produced a growing portfolio of quantified case studies across aerospace, automotive, energy, healthcare, financial services, and manufacturing that demonstrate specific outcomes rather than generic productivity claims.

Airbus represents one of Palantir's most significant commercial partnerships and best-quantified deployments. The aerospace manufacturer co-developed Skywise with Palantir, a data platform built on Foundry that serves the global aviation industry. Skywise processes more than 2 million flight parameters daily, integrating data from aircraft sensors, airline operations, manufacturing processes, and maintenance logs into a unified ontology. One airline using Skywise improved fleet utilization by 7 percentage points, a material improvement in an industry where even single-digit percentage gains translate to tens of millions in additional revenue. Airbus also uses Foundry internally for its own manufacturing processes and supply chain optimization, extending the partnership beyond customer-facing products into core operations. The scale of Skywise deployment across Airbus's airline customer base demonstrates how Foundry can serve as industry infrastructure rather than just a single-enterprise tool.

Stellantis provides another example of deep commercial penetration in automotive manufacturing. The automaker uses Foundry for supply chain management and resilience, improving visibility across complex multi-tier global supply chains that span thousands of suppliers across dozens of countries. The platform helps identify potential disruptions before they occur, optimizing inventory levels to reduce working capital while avoiding production stoppages. In early 2026, Palantir extended this partnership for five years, signaling sustained perceived value in a vertical where supply chain disruptions have caused billions in losses since 2020. Foundry applications at Stellantis include production scheduling, quality control optimization, and parts allocation during shortages, all operating over a shared ontology that connects manufacturing data with supplier networks.

Scuderia Ferrari illustrates how Palantir's technology serves extreme performance environments where milliseconds determine competitive outcomes. The Formula 1 team uses Palantir to rapidly analyze vast amounts of telemetry and sensor data from F1 cars and race simulations, processing data streams in real time during races to optimize car performance and strategy. In a sport where data overload is a constant challenge, the ability to surface actionable insights from thousands of sensor parameters provides genuine competitive advantage. Ferrari uses the platform for both race-day decision-making and longer-term engineering analysis, connecting data from the track with data from wind tunnels and simulations. This deployment demonstrates how Foundry's ontology can accommodate real-time telemetry processing alongside historical analysis in a domain where data latency directly translates to track performance.

Hertz has chosen AIP to modernize fleet analytics and AI development, replacing a mix of legacy systems that had accumulated through acquisitions and organic growth. The car rental company aims to optimize fleet allocation, pricing, and maintenance using generative AI and agents working over vehicle, customer, and operational data. For a fleet operator with hundreds of thousands of vehicles across thousands of locations, the ability to dynamically reposition assets based on demand signals represents significant revenue opportunity. AIP agents read telemetry data, pricing information, and booking demand in real time, then propose actions that planners can accept or override. This represents Palantir extending from pure analytics into what the company calls "autonomous margin management" across large, asset-heavy networks. Hertz's deployment follows a pattern seen in other fleet operators: the shift from batch planning to near-real-time optimization using agents that can propose operational decisions rather than merely displaying dashboards.

Kinder Morgan, one of the largest energy infrastructure companies in North America, uses Foundry to give gas control teams a unified real-time view of pipeline networks spanning tens of thousands of miles. The platform integrates data from sensors, compressors, storage facilities, and customer demand into a single operational picture. Tasks that used to take hours per day can now be done in minutes, and engineers can run simulations of outages or new loads directly on the model rather than relying on offline analysis. This capability transforms pipeline operations from reactive firefighting to predictive optimization, allowing operators to anticipate problems and test interventions before implementing them. The Kinder Morgan deployment illustrates Foundry's application in critical infrastructure where reliability and safety are paramount and where even minor efficiency improvements compound across massive physical networks.

BP provides an important counterexample that demonstrates the limits of Palantir's commercial appeal. The energy major historically used Palantir across upstream exploration and production, midstream pipelines, and downstream refining operations. However, BP notably scaled back its usage, representing a rare public example of a customer disengaging after initial adoption. This reduction indicates that perceived long-term value and cost considerations can shift over time, particularly in industries with strong internal engineering capabilities that may choose to build solutions in-house rather than pay ongoing software subscriptions. BP's experience raises questions about retention risk in Palantir's commercial business and suggests that not all deployments achieve the kind of compounding returns that produce long-term contract renewals. Understanding why BP scaled back, whether due to cost versus value calculations, internal build versus buy decisions, or competitive solutions, provides important context for assessing Palantir's commercial moat.

Jacobs uses Foundry and AIP for its OneWater platform, building predictive models for wastewater and drinking water systems. The platform turns a traditionally asset-heavy, low-tech domain into something that behaves like an industrial digital twin, enabling operators to predict overflows, plan preventive interventions, and optimize chemical dosing. Agents can propose pump setting changes or alert operators to upcoming shortages based on weather patterns, consumption forecasts, and equipment status. Water infrastructure represents a challenging domain for data platforms because assets are distributed, legacy systems are fragmented, and many utilities lack sophisticated analytics capabilities. Jacobs OneWater demonstrates how Foundry can serve as the technological foundation for managed service offerings, where Jacobs uses Palantir technology to deliver services to municipal water utilities that lack the resources to build such capabilities themselves.

Lowe's is working with Palantir to build a supply chain digital twin that optimizes inventory and replenishment, moving from batch planning to near real-time optimization across its retail network. The home improvement retailer operates hundreds of stores with millions of SKUs, creating a supply chain optimization problem of enormous complexity. The digital twin approach allows Lowe's to simulate the effects of potential changes before implementing them, testing how a new supplier, routing change, or inventory policy might affect service levels, carrying costs, and transportation expenses. This deployment represents a commercial example of an ontology plus agents managing a large logistics network, aligning with the "operational sovereignty" theme that runs through Palantir's most successful deployments. The Lowe's case also illustrates how retail operators, despite operating on thinner margins than energy or industrial companies, can justify investment in sophisticated optimization platforms when the potential efficiency gains are sufficiently large.

Financial services and insurance represent a growing vertical for AIP adoption. AIG expects to double its five-year compound annual growth rate partly due to Palantir technology, embedding agents into underwriting and claims workflows rather than just analytics dashboards. A major healthcare payer signed a five-year, $26 million deal within five weeks of an AIP trial, illustrating how compressed sales cycles and rapid trial-to-deal conversion are reshaping the ROI narrative. Applications in financial services include advanced fraud detection and prevention, anti-money laundering and know your customer compliance, risk management and capital adequacy, trading strategy optimization, and underwriting workflow automation. The value proposition is increasingly about embedding agents into core business processes that directly affect revenue or loss ratios, rather than peripheral analytics use cases. The expansion of Palantir's consulting partnership with Bain and Company illustrates a new channel strategy, where Bain embeds Palantir technology in its client engagements and serves as a force multiplier for commercial adoption across financial services and other verticals where consulting relationships provide trusted access.

Healthcare and life sciences beyond Tampa General Hospital include pharmaceutical giant Sanofi, which uses Foundry to accelerate R&D processes, optimize clinical trials, improve manufacturing quality and yield, and enhance supply chain visibility for pharmaceuticals. Drug development represents one of the most data-intensive and expensive industrial processes, with each late-stage clinical trial costing hundreds of millions and taking years to complete. Even modest acceleration in trial timelines or improvement in manufacturing yield can justify substantial software investment. Sanofi's deployment demonstrates how Foundry can serve across the pharmaceutical value chain, from early research through manufacturing and distribution, with a shared ontology connecting data from clinical trials, manufacturing plants, and supply chains. Tampa General Hospital's CareComm system, meanwhile, has produced some of the strongest published ROI numbers in healthcare: an 83 percent reduction in patient placement time, a 28 percent decrease in post-anesthesia care unit holds, and a 30 percent reduction in sepsis length of stay. The hospital won the Joint Commission's 2025 Healthcare Innovation Award partly for this work, validating Palantir's approach at institutional level.

The diversity of these commercial deployments reveals patterns in where Palantir succeeds and struggles. The strongest case studies share characteristics in common: complex, multi-step operations with many variables, data spread across fragmented systems, high cost of failure, and significant financial impact from marginal improvements. Airlines, automakers, pipeline operators, and hospitals all fit this profile. BP's scale-back suggests that companies with strong internal engineering cultures and substantial technology budgets may conclude they can build adequate solutions in-house rather than pay ongoing Palantir fees, particularly after the initial knowledge transfer from Forward Deployed Engineers creates internal expertise. The boot camp model partially addresses this by empowering users to build their own agents, but retention remains a key question mark as early commercial deployments age out of initial contract terms and into renewal decisions.

Time to first value has emerged as a critical factor in commercial ROI assessment. Pre-AIP deployments often required many months of ontology modeling and change management before visible ROI emerged, with organizations committing significant resources upfront and waiting months for proof of impact. AIP boot camps have fundamentally shifted this curve, with working agents built in days rather than months and users building workflows themselves rather than waiting for Forward Deployed Engineers. When organizations can see working agents on their own data within a week, the risk-reward calculus changes significantly. This compressed time to value explains the acceleration in commercial customer count from 143 in 2022 to 262 by Q1 2024 and the revenue growth exceeding 120 percent year over year by Q3 2025. However, the 5 to 10 percent conversion rate from boot camp to paying customer indicates that rapid demonstration does not guarantee long-term adoption, suggesting that many organizations experience value in the pilot phase but struggle to translate that into production-scale deployments worthy of multi-year contracts.

The cumulative compounding effect of ontology investment represents another ROI dimension often overlooked in individual case studies. Each deployment builds an enterprise ontology that can be reused for new agents and use cases without refactoring the foundations. The NHS Federated Data Platform figures illustrate this effect: millions of records processed, hundreds of thousands of patients discharged or removed from waiting lists. These outcomes result from many micro-workflows built on a shared ontology rather than a single hero application. This connects to the "Operational Sovereignty" concept: the ROI is not just one application but the ability to keep adding agents over time on stable foundations. Organizations that fully embrace this model gain compounding returns on their initial ontology investment, while those that treat each use case as a discrete project fail to capture this multiplier effect. Commercial customers like Airbus and Stellantis, with multi-year contract extensions, appear to be building these compound-return ontologies, while one-off deployments may never realize the full potential of the platform.

Front-line friction remains a challenge even when macro metrics look promising, as the NHS Federated Data Platform experience demonstrates. Freedom of Information requests and campaign groups such as "No Palantir in the NHS" have collected feedback from staff describing issues including limited adoption, poor usability, restricted data access, technical barriers for analysts, and significant staff time spent wrestling with the system. Even when macro-level metrics are impressive, there can be serious front-line friction and pockets of non-use that threaten long-term stickiness. This pattern likely extends to commercial deployments as well, where impressive pilot results may not translate to enterprise-wide adoption if the user experience fails to meet front-line worker expectations. BP's scale-back may reflect exactly this dynamic: impressive capabilities in theory but insufficient adoption in practice to justify ongoing subscription costs. Successful commercial deployments require investment in change management and user experience just as heavily as in the underlying technology, a reality that Palantir's boot camp model addresses only partially.

Vendor lock-in versus open interfaces represents a final strategic consideration for commercial customers. Large institutions risk deep lock-in when the enterprise ontology, agents, and workflows all live on one vendor's stack. Once an organization has invested years in building an ontology on Foundry, migrating away becomes technically and organizationally difficult. This dependency concern is particularly acute for public institutions but also matters for commercial enterprises that value strategic flexibility. Palantir has made some efforts to address this criticism, such as Merck making Foundry development tooling open source to attract more contributors and reduce single-vendor bottlenecks. However, the core ontology and agent infrastructure remain proprietary, and the tension between platform stickiness and customer independence continues to shape adoption decisions. Commercial customers must weigh the compounding returns from deep platform investment against the strategic risk of dependency on a single vendor whose pricing and roadmap they do not control.
