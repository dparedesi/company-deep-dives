## AIP economics and customer health

AIP has fundamentally reshaped Palantir's revenue model, moving the company from traditional seat-based licensing toward consumption-based pricing that aligns with how enterprises actually use AI infrastructure. Under the AIP model, customers contract for platform access across Foundry, AIP, and often Gotham or Apollo, then pay incrementally for AI inference and agentic workflows measured in tokens and compute-seconds. This shift mirrors how cloud providers monetize infrastructure, where baseline platform commitments establish a foundation and variable usage charges capture the value created as deployments scale. In practice, AIP revenue arrives as solution bundles tailored to vertical use cases: insurance underwriting, hospital operations, retail store optimization, or manufacturing quality control. These bundles combine fixed platform fees with variable compute charges, creating a hybrid model that provides predictable baseline revenue while allowing for significant expansion as customers integrate AI deeper into their operations. The vertical packaging strategy represents a meaningful evolution from Palantir's earlier generic platform approach, as pre-built workflows and industry-specific ontologies reduce implementation time and accelerate the path to first value.

The economics of AIP differ materially from Palantir's legacy Gotham and Foundry businesses. AIP deployments typically begin with boot camps, those intensive one to five day workshops where prospective customers bring their own data and problems to build working AI workflows alongside Palantir engineers. Boot camps operate as loss leaders in many cases, requiring Palantir to invest engineering time and compute resources before any contract is signed. The conversion rate from boot camp to paying customer is not disclosed, but the correlation between boot camp volume and US commercial revenue growth suggests the funnel is efficient. US commercial revenue grew 93% year over year in Q2 2025 and 121% in Q3 2025, periods during which boot camp activity accelerated significantly. Once converted, AIP customers often follow a familiar pattern: initial deployment in a single business unit or use case, followed by expansion to additional departments and workflows as value is demonstrated. The Walgreens deployment illustrates this trajectory, scaling from 10 stores to 4,000 in approximately eight months while automating hundreds of billions of micro-decisions per day. This expansion phase drives substantial revenue growth within existing accounts, a key reason why net dollar retention rates run above 120% for many commercial customers.

Compute margins represent a critical economic consideration for AIP that differs from traditional software. Unlike pure software products where incremental costs are minimal, AI inference requires GPU compute that Palantir must purchase from cloud providers. The spread between what Palantir charges customers for compute and what it pays to hyperscalers constitutes a critical margin lever. Management has indicated that compute margins remain healthy even as AIP scales, suggesting Palantir maintains pricing power relative to underlying infrastructure costs. The ability to package compute within vertical solutions rather than selling raw inference as a commodity contributes to margin preservation. Customers pay for outcomes, underwriting automation or factory optimization, rather than for GPU hours directly. This outcome-based pricing insulates Palantir from the commoditization pressure that affects infrastructure providers. The fixed-fee component of AIP contracts, typically tied to platform access rather than usage, further stabilizes margins by providing a baseline of high-margin software revenue that is not correlated with compute costs.

Annual contract values for AIP deployments vary significantly by customer size and segment, but several patterns emerge from disclosed deals. Large commercial customers often start in the low seven figures for initial AIP deployments, with expansion contracts reaching eight figures as use cases proliferate across the enterprise. Government AIP contracts, while less numerous, tend to be larger and longer in duration, reflecting the procurement cycles and mission-critical nature of the work. The shift to consumption-based pricing with AIP creates a different revenue profile compared to traditional multi-year fixed contracts. Rather than recognizing revenue evenly over a contract term, Palantir now recognizes revenue that fluctuates with customer usage. This creates variability quarter to quarter but also enables faster revenue capture as customers accelerate their AI adoption. The company's guidance methodology has evolved to account for this variability, with management raising guidance three times during FY2025 as AIP demand exceeded expectations. Full year 2025 revenue reached approximately 4.48 billion dollars, exceeding the guidance range of 4.396 to 4.40 billion, demonstrating the leverage that AIP provides to financial results when adoption accelerates.

The relationship between boot camps and revenue conversion operates as the primary engine of Palantir's commercial go-to-market motion. Boot camps function as both lead generation and product demonstration, compressing what traditionally required months of evaluation into days of intensive work. Prospects arrive with actual datasets and leave with working workflows, a dramatic departure from the slide-ware demos that characterize much of enterprise software sales. This product-led approach shortens sales cycles and improves conversion rates because prospects experience value rather than hearing about it. The boot camp model also scales more efficiently than Palantir's traditional high-touch consulting approach. Rather than deploying Forward Deployed Engineers for months-long engagements, a single boot camp can serve multiple prospective customers simultaneously. The capital efficiency of this model is reflected in Palantir's sales and marketing economics, with revenue growth outpacing sales investment as the boot camp motion matures. US commercial customer count has grown from approximately 100 customers to more than 600, a six-fold increase that coincides with the boot camp strategy becoming the core commercial motion.

Net dollar retention provides the most revealing window into customer expansion dynamics. Palantir's NDR typically runs between 115% and 120%, meaning existing customers generate 15% to 20% more revenue year over year even before new customer acquisition is factored in. This retention rate exceeds the typical SaaS benchmark of 110% and reflects the strength of the land-and-expand model. The AIG deployment exemplifies how NDR translates in practice, with the insurer rebuilding its underwriting process on AIP and effectively multiplying underwriter capacity with reported productivity gains of two to five times. Such transformative deployments create their own expansion momentum as successful use cases catalyze adoption across additional business units. NDR varies by segment, with commercial customers typically showing higher expansion rates than government customers. Government NDR runs closer to 110% reflecting more contractually fixed revenue and slower expansion cycles, while commercial NDR can exceed 125% for customers undergoing digital transformation initiatives across their enterprises. The consistency of NDR above 110% across both segments indicates that Palantir's platform successfully creates switching costs and ongoing value regardless of customer type.

Customer concentration has improved meaningfully over Palantir's history, reducing dependency on any single counterparty. In the early years, a small number of government contracts represented a substantial portion of revenue. By 2025, no single customer accounted for more than 3% of total revenue, according to company disclosures. This diversification reduces risk and provides more predictable revenue as individual contract renewals or losses have diminished impact on overall results. The top 10 customers now represent a smaller percentage of revenue than in the early 2020s, even as absolute customer count has grown. Government revenue concentration remains higher than commercial concentration given the nature of defense and intelligence contracting, where individual programs can reach hundreds of millions of dollars. However, even within government, Palantir has broadened its customer base across more agencies and international allies, mitigating the risk associated with any single government budget cycle. Commercial revenue concentration has decreased particularly rapidly as the customer base expanded from approximately 100 to more than 600 customers in the US alone. The growth in customer count combined with stable or increasing average deal size indicates that Palantir is successfully penetrating smaller mid-market customers while maintaining its presence among large enterprises.

Customer count growth trajectories diverge significantly by segment. US commercial customer count grew from approximately 100 to more than 600 in the period following AIP's launch, representing a six-fold increase that underscores the boot camp motion's effectiveness. International commercial customer growth has lagged, contributing to the relative underperformance of international commercial revenue compared to US commercial. Government customer count has grown more slowly, which is expected given the longer sales cycles and smaller total addressable market of government agencies versus commercial enterprises. However, government customer expansion has accelerated internationally as allied nations adopt Palantir platforms for defense and intelligence applications. The average government deal size significantly exceeds the average commercial deal size, with individual government contracts often reaching tens or hundreds of millions of dollars. Commercial deal sizes range from six figures for mid-market companies to eight figures for large enterprises undertaking multi-year digital transformations. The distribution of deal sizes creates a healthy revenue mix where commercial customers provide growth and volume while government customers provide stability and high-value contracts.

Churn has been relatively rare among Palantir's customer base, but notable examples provide insight into the competitive dynamics and risks. BP, the energy giant, represented a publicly cited customer loss when the company ended its Palantir engagement in 2023 following a multi-year deployment. The BP case illustrates several risk factors: Palantir's high pricing can become difficult to justify during corporate cost-cutting initiatives, and some customers may attempt to build internal capabilities rather than paying for external platforms. The energy sector has also developed competing platforms that can siphon away potential customers. However, BP appears to be an exception rather than the rule. Palantir's disclosed churn rates remain significantly below typical SaaS benchmarks, with many customers maintaining relationships for five, seven, or even ten years. The combination of deep data integration, custom ontologies, and mission-critical workflows creates substantial switching costs that protect against churn. Even when customers reduce spending or pause expansions, existing relationships often continue rather than ending completely. The government customer base shows particularly low churn given the mission-critical nature of defense and intelligence work, where changing platforms would require re-certification and substantial operational disruption.

Rule of 40 performance provides a composite view of growth and margin efficiency, and Palantir has emerged as an exceptional performer on this metric. The Rule of 40 calculates revenue growth rate plus profit margin, typically using free cash flow margin or adjusted EBITDA margin. Scores above 40 indicate healthy software companies, while scores above 60 indicate exceptional performers. Palantir's Rule of 40 score has accelerated from approximately 55 in 2023 to 69 in 2024 to above 100 in 2025. This dramatic improvement reflects both accelerating revenue growth and expanding margins. In Q2 2025, with 29% revenue growth and approximately 40% free cash flow margin, the Rule of 40 score reached approximately 69. By Q3 2025, with revenue growth above 50% and free cash flow margin near 47%, the score exceeded 100. Achieving such scores at multi-billion dollar revenue scale is unusual, as most software companies see their Rule of 40 scores decline as they grow larger. Palantir's improving score at increasing scale suggests strong competitive positioning and widening moats. The Rule of 40 performance also validates the AIP strategy, demonstrating that the shift to consumption-based pricing has not compromised margins. Palantir is combining growth rates typical of early-stage companies with margins typical of mature software franchises, a combination that defines top-tier software businesses.
