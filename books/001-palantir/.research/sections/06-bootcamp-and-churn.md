## The boot camp engine and its limits

Since late 2023, Palantir has fundamentally shifted its commercial go-to-market motion through AIP boot camps. These free, intensive workshops run for three to five days and bring customer teams together with Palantir Forward Deployed Engineers to build working AI agents on the customer's actual data. The model represents a departure from traditional enterprise software sales. Rather than months of sales presentations and RFP processes, organizations now see working agents on their own data within a week.

The numbers behind this acceleration are substantial. Palantir ran approximately 324 boot camps in Q4 2023. By Q1 2024, that figure had risen to 660 boot camps in a single quarter. This volume has directly correlated with commercial customer growth. US commercial customer count rose from 143 at the end of 2022 to 221 by the end of 2023 and reached 262 by Q1 2024. The trajectory has continued upward, with Palantir reporting more than 600 US commercial customers by 2025.

Boot camps compress what was once a multi-month or multi-year sales cycle into days. The traditional enterprise software model involved lengthy procurement processes, extended proof-of-concept periods, and gradual rollout phases. Palantir's boot camp model inverts this. Customers arrive with their own data engineers and domain experts. Over several intensive days, they build working agents that address specific business problems. By the end of the week, they have something that functions on their data rather than a slide deck about what might be possible.

The economics of this model rely on conversion rates that would concern traditional sales organizations but work for Palantir at scale. Early data indicates that 5 to 10 percent of boot camp participants convert into paying customers within 3 to 6 months. This看似 low conversion rate is offset by the low cost of running a boot camp compared to traditional enterprise sales cycles. More importantly, customers who convert have already experienced success with their own data, dramatically reducing implementation risk.

Forward Deployed Engineers play a crucial role in the boot camp model but in a different capacity than in Palantir's traditional deployments. Historically, FDEs would embed with customers for months or years, building solutions on behalf of the client. In the boot camp model, FDEs facilitate and teach rather than doing all the work themselves. Customer teams build their own agents with guidance. This shift has important implications for Palantir's cost structure and scalability. The company can serve more prospects with the same number of FDEs, and customers gain skills that make them more self-sufficient over time.

The revenue impact of this model has been dramatic. US commercial revenue grew approximately 40 percent year over year through 2023 and early 2024. By Q3 2025, US commercial revenue growth exceeded 120 percent year over year. Remaining deal value, a metric representing contracted but not yet recognized revenue, nearly tripled. This indicates that boot camps are not just generating small pilots but substantial commercial commitments.

Time to first value has collapsed as a result of the boot camp model. Pre-AIP deployments often required many months of ontology modeling and data preparation before visible ROI emerged. Organizations committed significant resources upfront with delayed proof of impact. AIP boot camps have fundamentally shifted this curve. Working agents can be built in days. Users themselves build workflows rather than waiting for Forward Deployed Engineers to develop every solution. Short trials convert quickly into multi-year deals.

A major healthcare payer's path to deployment illustrates this compressed timeline. The organization signed a five-year, $26 million deal within five weeks of an AIP trial. In traditional enterprise software sales, a deal of that size might require six to twelve months of procurement, negotiation, and technical evaluation. The boot camp model enabled the payer to validate the technology on its own data and workflows at minimal cost before committing to a substantial contract.

The boot camp model also changes the conversation around cost and value. Traditional enterprise software pricing often requires substantial upfront investment with uncertain returns. Palantir's boot camps are free. Customers can see working agents on their own data before writing a check. This reduces procurement friction and enables line-of-business teams to validate the technology before involving central purchasing functions. The risk-reward calculus shifts dramatically when the initial investment is time rather than capital.

However, the boot camp model creates new challenges that Palantir must navigate. With 660 boot camps in a single quarter, even a 5 to 10 percent conversion rate generates more new customers than Palantir can traditionally support with heavy FDE deployments. The company has had to evolve its customer success model to serve more customers with fewer resources per account. This puts pressure on product usability and self-service capabilities. If boot camps generate customers who expect heavy FDE involvement that Palantir can no longer provide, churn risk increases.

The model also creates pipeline visibility challenges. Traditional enterprise software sales involve clear stages from prospect to qualified lead to negotiation to close. Boot camps create a broad top of funnel with many participants but less predictable conversion timing. Palantir must forecast which boot camp participants will convert and when without relying on traditional sales stage progression. This has implications for revenue guidance and resource allocation.

Despite these challenges, the boot camp model has proven effective at accelerating commercial adoption. The growth from 143 to 262 US commercial customers in roughly fifteen months represents faster customer acquisition than Palantir achieved in any comparable period as a public company. The acceleration to more than 600 customers by 2025 suggests the model continues to scale. Remaining deal value nearly tripling provides confidence that boot camp conversions are translating into meaningful commercial relationships rather than one-off projects.

## Churn, deployment friction, and the POC theatre risk

Not every Palantir deployment succeeds, and public examples of churn or scale-backs provide important context for understanding the platform's limits. BP notably scaled back its Palantir usage, representing a rare public example of customer disengagement. The reduction indicates that perceived long-term value and cost considerations can lead customers to reduce or eliminate Palantir deployments even after initial adoption. Understanding why BP scaled back would provide important context for retention risk, though the specific reasons have not been publicly disclosed.

The BP case may reflect several dynamics. Cost versus value calculations can shift as initial contracts renew and pricing increases. Large energy companies may have sufficient internal engineering capability to consider building rather than buying. Competing solutions from hyperscalers or specialized vendors may offer "good enough" functionality at lower price points. Or the deployment may have failed to reach production scale, remaining stuck in pilot purgatory until executive patience expired. Whatever the specific cause, the BP scale-back demonstrates that Palantir contracts are not immune to cancellation or reduction.

The NHS Federated Data Platform illustrates a different kind of challenge. Despite impressive macro-level metrics, including approximately 80,900 additional patients treated in theatres and 77,200 patients safely removed from inpatient waiting lists, front-line staff have reported significant friction. Freedom of Information requests and campaign groups such as "No Palantir in the NHS" have collected feedback describing limited adoption, poor usability, restricted data access, technical barriers for analysts, and significant staff time spent wrestling with the system.

This disconnect between macro metrics and micro experience poses a strategic risk. Hospital executives see system-level benefits and renewed contracts. Front-line clinicians and administrators experience frustration and resistance. The British Medical Association voted in 2025 to oppose the rollout and lobby for termination of existing Palantir contracts, citing privacy, governance, and trust concerns. A 2024 BMJ editorial titled "NHS England must cancel its contract with Palantir" exemplifies how even measurable operational gains are being weighed against perceived sovereignty and ethical risks.

The tension between system-level value and user-level friction affects retention and expansion. Even when contracts renew, disgruntled front-line users can limit the depth of adoption. Expansion into new use cases becomes more difficult when existing users are skeptical. Word of mouth within industries can slow new customer acquisition. The NHS experience demonstrates that quantified ROI at the executive level does not guarantee smooth adoption at the user level.

The boot camp model itself creates a new risk that Palantir management has acknowledged: POC theatre. With hundreds of boot camps per quarter, impressive demos may never scale to production. The term "POC theatre" refers to pilots that look compelling in demonstration but fail to integrate into core workflows or reach operational scale. Palantir runs 660 boot camps per quarter, but only 5 to 10 percent convert to paying customers within 3 to 6 months. The remaining 90 to 95 percent represent failed or stalled evaluations.

This conversion funnel is not inherently problematic. Many software companies evaluate ten prospects to close one deal. The risk is when impressive demos create expectations that subsequent deployments cannot meet. Customers who experienced a smooth boot camp may expect production rollout to be equally straightforward. When reality involves data governance debates, change management challenges, or integration complexities, disappointment can set in. The gap between demo and production can breed skepticism even among customers who ultimately deploy successfully.

Agent sprawl represents an emerging governance challenge as AIP adoption accelerates. Organizations that run multiple boot camps across different business units may accumulate dozens or hundreds of agents without centralized oversight. One division might build agents for supply chain optimization, another for customer service, another for financial planning. These agents may operate on inconsistent data definitions, make conflicting recommendations, or duplicate functionality. Without governance frameworks, agent proliferation can create new silos rather than breaking down existing ones.

The governance challenge extends beyond individual organizations. Palantir's emphasis on "operational sovereignty" means customers maintain control of their data and ontology. But as agents proliferate across an enterprise, questions arise about who owns which agents, how they are validated, and how conflicts are resolved. A procurement agent optimized for cost reduction might conflict with a quality agent optimized for supplier reliability. Without oversight, agent interactions can create operational problems that are difficult to diagnose and resolve.

Net dollar retention rates provide a window into customer health, though Palantir does not disclose specific figures. The company reports strong NDR as evidence that customers expand usage over time. Remaining deal value nearly tripling suggests that existing customers are signing larger commitments. However, the BP scale-back and the potential for agent sprawl-related churn highlight that NDR can mask underlying churn dynamics. Customers who expand in one division while pulling back in another may show positive NDR while experiencing deployment complexity.

The commercial customer growth trajectory from 143 (2022) to 262 (Q1 2024) to more than 600 (2025) demonstrates that net new customer acquisition exceeds churn. But the churn rate itself remains undisclosed. In enterprise software, net dollar retention above 100 percent can coexist with meaningful logo churn if expanding customers more than offset those who depart. Palantir's strong NDR and customer growth suggest that departures like BP are exceptions rather than the rule, but the lack of public churn data makes precise assessment difficult.

The sales cycle compression that boot camps enable also creates retention risk. Traditional enterprise software sales involving lengthy evaluation periods create sunk costs and relationship investment before deployment begins. Customers who spend months evaluating and procuring software have incentive to make it work. A five-week sales cycle, as seen with the healthcare payer who signed a $26 million deal, reduces this sunk cost effect. If the initial deployment encounters challenges, the customer has less invested and may be more willing to discontinue.

Palantir's evolution from heavy FDE-led deployments to boot camp-driven customer self-service therefore creates both opportunity and risk. The opportunity lies in faster customer acquisition, compressed time to value, and scalable economics. The risk lies in customers who adopt quickly but abandon quickly when implementations encounter friction. The company's ability to retain customers will increasingly depend on product usability and customer success at scale rather than the deep consulting relationships that characterized its earlier years.

The churn and deployment challenge landscape ultimately reflects Palantir's transition from a consulting-heavy services business to a product-led software company. Boot camps and AIP enable faster adoption, but they also reduce the margin for error in implementation. Front-line friction at the NHS, scale-backs at BP, and the risk of POC theatre all point to the same reality: impressive technology and strong macro metrics are necessary but not sufficient for long-term customer retention. Change management, user experience, and governance mechanisms determine whether boot camp converts become lasting customers or impressive pilots that never reach production scale.
