## The NHS data battleground

Palantir's entry into the United Kingdom's National Health Service represents one of the most sustained and intense ethical controversies in the company's history. In 2023, NHS England awarded Palantir a contract to build and operate the Federated Data Platform, a system designed to integrate patient data across the health service for purposes including waiting list management, care coordination, and operational efficiency. The contract, initially valued at approximately STG 330 million over seven years with a potential extension to STG 480 million, placed a US defense contractor at the heart of Britain's most cherished public institution. The FDP went live in late 2023 and began rolling out to NHS trusts across England in 2024, shifting the controversy from theoretical concerns about a potential contract to concrete resistance from within the medical profession.

The British Medical Journal, one of the world's oldest and most respected medical journals, entered the fray in November 2024 with an editorial titled "NHS England must cancel its contract with Palantir." The BMJ editorial board argued that the partnership risked eroding public trust in health data systems and recommended outright cancellation of the contract. This was not a marginal activist publication but a cornerstone of the British medical establishment, dating to 1840 with a global readership of physicians and researchers. The editorial cited concerns about data sovereignty, the appropriateness of a company linked to immigration enforcement and military work holding sensitive patient data, and the lack of transparent public consultation before the award. The BMJ's position represented a significant escalation of professional medical opposition from scattered criticism to an institutional stance.

In 2025, the British Medical Association's Annual Representative Meeting passed a formal motion declaring Palantir an unacceptable partner for the FDP. The BMA is the trade union representing doctors in the United Kingdom, with more than 150,000 members. The motion instructed the union to lobby against Palantir's involvement in the NHS and to support clinicians who refuse to use the system. This was not a resolution by a peripheral medical specialty but the collective voice of the UK's doctors, including general practitioners, hospital consultants, and trainees. The BMA had never previously taken such a position on a technology vendor, making the Palantir motion an extraordinary intervention that signaled how far medical opposition had moved beyond privacy advocacy groups into the core of the profession.

Campaign groups including No Palantir in the NHS and Corporate Watch used Freedom of Information requests to compile evidence about FDP rollout and adoption. Their findings painted a picture of patchy implementation, with questions about usability, uptake rates, and staff burden at individual trusts. FOI responses revealed that some NHS trusts had declined to participate in the FDP or had implemented only limited components, while others reported difficulties with training and interface design. The federated model, which in theory allows local trusts to maintain control of their data while connecting to a wider platform, in practice created variable adoption patterns. Campaign groups argued that NHS England's public statements about benefits and uptake did not match the on-the-ground reality documented through FOI disclosures.

NHS England's own benefits dashboard reported that hundreds of thousands of patients had been removed from waiting lists through FDP-enabled deduplication and that administrative delays had been reduced through better data integration. These operational gains represented the core value proposition that convinced NHS leaders to proceed with Palantir despite the controversy. Waiting lists for elective care had reached record highs in the aftermath of the COVID-19 pandemic, with more than 7 million patients waiting for treatment at the peak of the crisis. Any system that could help manage this backlog had genuine humanitarian value. The tension lay between demonstrable operational improvements and the symbolic and substantive concerns about entrusting Britain's health data to a foreign company with defense contracts.

Palantir won the NHS contract through a competitive tender process against rivals including Epic, an established electronic health record company, and several domestic technology providers. The company had previously worked with NHS England on pandemic response efforts, including the vaccine rollout program, which provided a track record in healthcare operations. Palantir's Foundry platform had been used during the COVID-19 response to coordinate data across the NHS, creating what the company argued was proof of capability in sensitive healthcare contexts. The FDP contract award was defended by NHS leaders on the grounds that Palantir offered the best technical solution and that robust data protection arrangements were in place, including contractual guarantees that Palantir would not own or monetize NHS patient data.

The resistance from the medical establishment reflected deeper concerns about the long-term implications of the Palantir relationship. Doctors and medical ethicists worried that the FDP could become a "national health surveillance backbone," a phrase that appeared in critical analyses of the contract. The same data integration that enabled better care coordination could theoretically be used for purposes far removed from direct patient care, including resource allocation decisions, performance management that penalized struggling institutions, or even immigration enforcement data requests. While Palantir and NHS England maintained that legal safeguards prevented such function creep, the absence of detailed public technical specifications made independent verification difficult.

Public consent for large-scale health data use operates as a precondition for digitally enabled health systems. The NHS had previously faced public backlash over data sharing initiatives, most notably the care.data program which was abandoned in 2016 after public outcry about how patient information would be used. The care.data failure demonstrated that even technically sound data initiatives could collapse without public trust. The controversy over Palantir's role raised the risk that the FDP could face a similar crisis of legitimacy, particularly if high-profile medical institutions like the BMA continued to campaign against it. The operational benefits of improved data integration could not be realized if patients and clinicians refused to participate in the system.

What made the NHS controversy distinct from Palantir's other ethical battles was its institutionalization within the medical profession. ICE opposition came from human rights organizations and immigration activists. Israel-Gaza criticism came from NGOs and some institutional investors. NHS opposition came from the British Medical Journal and the British Medical Association, the very bodies that speak for and to the doctors expected to use Palantir's software. This created a different kind of pressure, one that could affect day-to-day implementation through passive resistance, low adoption rates, or outright refusal by clinicians to engage with the system. The BMA's pledge to support clinicians who refuse to use FDP suggested that the controversy could move from political protest to practical obstruction within NHS trusts.

The NHS contract also carried implications for Palantir's broader European commercial ambitions. Winning the FDP was intended as a flagship deal that would demonstrate Palantir's value in healthcare and public sector contexts beyond defense and intelligence. A successful NHS deployment would provide a powerful case study for other national health systems and government agencies considering Palantir's technology. Conversely, a high-profile failure or public rupture with the medical establishment would damage those prospects. The stakes of the NHS controversy extended well beyond Britain, affecting how public sector clients across Europe and elsewhere viewed Palantir as a partner for sensitive civilian government work.

## GDPR, the EU AI Act, and data sovereignty

European data regulation represents a structured, legalistic form of the ethical controversies that Palantir faces globally. The General Data Protection Regulation, which took full effect in 2018, established comprehensive rules for the processing of personal data within the European Union and the European Economic Area. Palantir's platforms, which integrate and analyze datasets that often contain personal information, must comply with GDPR requirements including data minimization, purpose limitation, access controls, and the right to erasure. The company maintains that its technical architecture includes granular privacy protections, audit trails, and governance features designed to help clients meet their GDPR obligations. However, the gap between claimed technical compliance and demonstrable compliance in practice remains a point of contention.

Dutch privacy advocacy group SOMI has called for EU-wide investigations into Palantir's data processing practices. SOMI, which operates under the full name Stichting Privacy First, has raised questions about how Palantir handles Europeans' data and whether its deployments meet GDPR requirements. The group has explored potential collective legal actions, signaling that privacy concerns about Palantir are moving from advocacy to litigation. Unlike ad-hoc criticism, SOMI's campaign represents a coordinated European privacy effort with the potential to result in regulatory complaints, enforcement actions, or class-style litigation. The involvement of established European privacy organizations indicates that GDPR concerns about Palantir have moved beyond theoretical discussion to concrete legal risk.

The EU AI Act, which entered into force in August 2024, creates a new regulatory layer that directly affects Palantir's business. The Act establishes a risk-based framework for AI systems, with "high risk" categories including law enforcement, border control, critical infrastructure, and healthcare. Many of Palantir's deployments fall squarely within these high-risk designations. The implementation timeline is phased, with prohibitions on certain AI practices and AI literacy obligations applying from February 2025, general purpose AI and governance rules from August 2025, and full high-risk system obligations phasing in through 2027. This staggered timeline creates both transition complexity and risk that Palantir or its clients may fall out of compliance as different provisions come into force at different dates.

High-risk AI systems under the EU AI Act must undergo conformity assessment, be registered in an EU database, meet strict data governance requirements, and provide transparency to users about the system's operation. These requirements directly challenge aspects of Palantir's business model, particularly in national security and law enforcement contexts where operational security traditionally limits transparency. The Act's emphasis on explainability and human oversight creates tension with Palantir's work on military AI systems like Maven Smart System, where the "black box" nature of some AI components is inherent to their function. The company will need to demonstrate that its deployments meet European standards without compromising capabilities that its defense and intelligence clients consider essential.

GDPR enforcement actions against Palantir have not yet resulted in major public fines, but the threat of enforcement shapes the company's European operations. Data protection authorities in individual EU countries can investigate companies operating within their jurisdictions, and cross-border cooperation mechanisms allow coordinated enforcement across the bloc. A significant GDPR fine against Palantir would not only carry financial penalties but would also create reputational damage and potentially trigger contract terminations by public sector clients sensitive to compliance risk. The absence of major public enforcement to date does not guarantee safety, as GDPR investigations can take years to result in action and privacy regulators often prioritize large, symbolic cases that establish precedents.

Data localization requirements add another layer of complexity to Palantir's European operations. Several EU countries have implemented or are considering rules that require certain types of data, particularly health and government data, to be stored and processed within national borders. These localization requirements conflict with Palantir's cloud-agnostic architecture and its ability to deploy across multiple infrastructure environments. The Federated Data Platform in the UK, which NHS England repeatedly emphasized would keep data within the UK, demonstrates how data sovereignty concerns can constrain deployment options. Similar requirements in other European countries could limit Palantir's ability to offer standardized solutions or could require costly local infrastructure investments.

The tension between Palantir's American defense connections and European data sovereignty concerns creates a geopolitical dimension to regulatory risk. Palantir's "Western hard power" doctrine, articulated by CEO Alex Karp, frames the company's work as supporting Western democracies and their allies. However, European regulators and governments increasingly push for "digital sovereignty" or "technological sovereignty," reducing dependence on non-European technology vendors for critical infrastructure. Palantir, despite its positioning as a supporter of Western democracies, is ultimately an American company with headquarters in Denver and Colorado. The EU's push for strategic autonomy includes reducing reliance on US technology providers, particularly for sensitive government and critical infrastructure applications. Palantir's defense contracts with the US Department of Defense and its support for Israeli military operations make some European institutions hesitant about deep integration, even as the company emphasizes its alignment with Western democratic values.

The EU AI Act's restrictions on high-risk AI systems in law enforcement and migration create specific challenges for Palantir's Gotham platform, which is used by police and border agencies across Europe. Predictive policing systems, risk assessment tools for border security, and migration management software all fall under enhanced scrutiny. The Act prohibits certain AI practices in migration contexts, including emotion recognition and the use of risk scores based on demographic profiling. Palantir must ensure that its deployments do not run afoul of these prohibitions, even when clients request capabilities that the Act forbids. This creates a dilemma where refusing client requests may lose business, but fulfilling them may violate EU law and expose the company to enforcement action.

Corporate governance adds another regulatory dimension. Palantir's insider selling patterns, including approximately $570 million in stock sales by co-founder Peter Thiel and substantial sales by other executives, have drawn scrutiny from European investors with governance mandates. Environmental, social, and governance investing is more established in Europe than in the United States, and European institutional investors often apply stricter ESG criteria when making investment decisions. Storebrand's 2024 divestment from Palantir over human rights concerns related to Israel and Gaza illustrates how ESG considerations can translate into concrete capital allocation decisions. A company's ethical profile, including its human rights record and governance practices, affects its access to European capital markets as much as its regulatory compliance affects its operational access.

Looking forward, the EU AI Act represents a potential template that other jurisdictions may adopt. The United Kingdom, having left the EU, is developing its own AI regulatory framework that may draw inspiration from the EU approach while diverging in specific provisions. Other democracies, including Canada and Japan, are considering AI regulation with similar risk-based approaches. Palantir's ability to navigate European regulation may prove predictive of its ability to operate in a global regulatory environment that is becoming more restrictive of high-risk AI applications. The company's "Operational Sovereignty" marketing, which emphasizes giving clients full control of their AI stack, runs into the paradox that European governments want both sovereign control and regulatory constraints on what that sovereignty can legally entail.

The GDPR and EU AI Act controversies differ from Palantir's other ethical battles in that they are not disputes about what the company should do morally, but legally enforceable requirements about what it must do. The difference between ethical persuasion and legal compliance is significant. Palantir can choose to ignore or reject ethical arguments about its ICE contracts or military work, continuing what it views as lawful and necessary business. It cannot ignore GDPR enforcement or EU AI Act compliance without risking fines, contract loss, or market exclusion. European regulation therefore represents the most structured, predictable, and potentially consequential form of the ethical challenges that Palantir faces. The question is not whether Palantir's work is right or wrong in the abstract, but whether it can adapt to a European regulatory environment that increasingly views data and AI as matters of public interest requiring democratic oversight rather than purely commercial arrangements.
