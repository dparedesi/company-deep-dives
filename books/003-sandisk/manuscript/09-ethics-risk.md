# Ethics, Controversies, and Risks

SanDisk Corporation's ethical profile presents a study in contrasts. The company demonstrated genuine leadership in supply chain responsibility, ranking third among 24 consumer electronics companies in the Enough Project's 2012 conflict minerals survey. This achievement placed SanDisk ahead of most major technology companies in responsible sourcing of minerals from conflict-affected regions. Co-founder Eli Harari received the National Medal of Technology and Innovation from President Obama in 2014, recognizing fundamental contributions to flash memory technology that enabled the mobile revolution. However, the Western Digital acquisition era from 2016 to 2025 brought multiple product controversies, security failures, and customer trust issues that tarnished this ethical foundation. The company also faces structural risks inherent to the NAND flash business that no amount of corporate responsibility can address: extreme commodity cyclicality, manufacturing concentration in geologically and geopolitically risky locations, and competitive pressure from Asian manufacturers with state-backed scale. The February 2025 spin-off that returned Sandisk to independence also recreated the risk profile that had necessitated the 2016 acquisition in the first place.

## Supply Chain Leadership and Conflict Minerals Sourcing

The strongest element of SanDisk's ethical record lies in supply chain responsibility, specifically around conflict minerals sourcing. In 2012, the Enough Project ranked SanDisk third best of 24 consumer electronics companies surveyed for progress on conflict minerals. This ranking covered company practices regarding sourcing of tin, tantalum, tungsten, and gold, known collectively as 3TG minerals, from the Democratic Republic of Congo region. The Enough Project is a human rights organization focused on exposing connections between conflict minerals and human rights abuses, including funding for armed groups in eastern Congo.

To understand the significance of this ranking, consider the context. The Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act, passed in 2010, included Section 1502 requiring companies to disclose whether their products contain conflict minerals originating from the Democratic Republic of Congo or adjoining countries. This disclosure requirement aimed to reduce funding for armed groups perpetrating violence in the region. However, compliance varied widely across the technology industry. Many companies issued generic statements about responsible sourcing without taking concrete action to trace mineral origins or support conflict-free supply chains.

SanDisk's third-place ranking in the Enough Project survey indicated concrete action beyond minimum compliance. The company demonstrated progress on four key dimensions: tracing minerals to smelters and mines, independent third-party audits, supporting conflict-free mining initiatives in Congo, and leading industry efforts to address the problem. This performance placed SanDisk ahead of major technology companies including Apple, Microsoft, and Intel, suggesting that conflict minerals sourcing was treated as a genuine ethical priority rather than merely a compliance exercise.

The practical implications of this leadership matter. Mineral tracing requires supply chain visibility that extends through multiple tiers: from mining operations to trading houses to smelters to refiners to component manufacturers to final product assembly. Establishing this tracing capability requires investment in supply chain management systems, supplier relationships, and verification processes. SanDisk's ability to implement these systems while managing costs in a commodity-competitive industry demonstrated that ethical sourcing and business viability are not mutually exclusive.

The impact of this leadership extends beyond SanDisk's own supply chain. As a major flash memory manufacturer, SanDisk supplied components to numerous OEM customers. Its commitment to conflict-free sourcing created ripple effects through the electronics supply chain, putting pressure on competitors to match its standards and providing conflict-free components to customers who wanted to avoid controversy. The ranking also gave SanDisk a marketing differentiator in ethically-conscious consumer and enterprise segments.

However, the durability of this commitment through corporate changes remains uncertain. The Enough Project ranking dates from 2012, during SanDisk's independent period. Western Digital's acquisition in 2016 and the 2025 spin-off may have disrupted supply chain management systems and shifted priorities. Publicly available information on Sandisk Corporation's post-2025 conflict minerals practices was not accessible in our research, creating a data gap on whether this ethical leadership has been maintained through corporate transitions.

## Technology Recognition and Innovation Ethics

Eli Harari's receipt of the National Medal of Technology and Innovation from President Barack Obama in 2014 represents another dimension of SanDisk's positive ethical profile. This recognition honored Harari's contributions to the development and commercialization of flash memory technology, specifically his work on Floating Gate EEPROM which proved the practicality, reliability, and endurance of semiconductor-based data storage.

The ethical significance of this recognition lies in its recognition of technology that serves human needs rather than exploits human weaknesses. Flash memory enabled the mobile revolution, transforming how people capture, store, and access information. The technology contributed to digital camera proliferation, smartphone adoption, portable computing, and countless applications that empower individuals. This is technology that expands human capability rather than enabling surveillance, manipulation, or exploitation.

Harari's personal background adds ethical dimension to the story. Born in 1945 in Tel Aviv to Polish Jewish parents who immigrated to Mandatory Palestine in 1933, Harari's family history reflected the upheavals of 20th-century Europe. His path from Princeton University graduate studies to semiconductor innovation at Hughes Aircraft, Intel, and eventually SanDisk represented the classic American immigrant entrepreneurial narrative. The National Medal recognized not just technical achievement but the broader narrative of immigrant contributions to American technological leadership.

The patent portfolio associated with Harari, Sanjay Mehrotra, and other SanDisk innovators represents substantial intellectual property developed through legitimate innovation rather than acquisition or appropriation. SanDisk generated licensing revenue from its extensive flash memory patent portfolio, providing a high-margin revenue stream that offset commodity cyclicality in product sales. This licensing revenue model rewarded genuine innovation and created incentives for continued R&D investment.

However, the ethical dimension of patent licensing in technology industries merits scrutiny. Patent portfolios can be used strategically to extract rents from competitors or block legitimate innovation. Patent trolls acquire patents solely for litigation rather than product development. SanDisk's approach appeared more balanced, using patents to protect innovations developed through substantial R&D investment while also licensing technology to enable industry growth. However, the company engaged in patent disputes with Samsung and other flash memory manufacturers, and the specific terms and outcomes of these disputes were not accessible in our research.

Sanjay Mehrotra's subsequent career adds another dimension to the ethical profile. After leaving SanDisk following the Western Digital acquisition, Mehrotra became CEO of Micron Technology in May 2017. Under his leadership, Micron continued investing in advanced memory technology while navigating the same commodity cycles and competitive pressures that characterize the NAND and DRAM markets. Mehrotra's election to the National Academy of Engineering in 2022 for contributions to nonvolatile memory design and architecture reinforced recognition of genuine technical achievement. However, his role at Micron also positions him as a competitor to Sandisk Corporation in the NAND market, creating complex competitive dynamics between the companies that once shared leadership.

## Western Digital Era Controversies: Product Marketing and Disclosure

The Western Digital acquisition era from 2016 to 2025 introduced multiple controversies that contrast sharply with SanDisk's pre-acquisition ethical record. These controversies primarily involved product marketing practices, disclosure issues, and security failures. While these incidents occurred under Western Digital ownership, they affected products and customer relationships that transferred to Sandisk Corporation through the 2025 spin-off, creating ethical and reputational liabilities for the re-emerged independent company.

The WD Green drives controversy emerged in 2007, before Western Digital acquired SanDisk but illustrating a pattern of misleading marketing that would continue through the WD era. Western Digital advertised WD GP (Green Power) drives with rotational speed "between 7200 and 5400 rpm." This claim was technically accurate but fundamentally misleading. The drives actually spun at 5405 rpm with variable spin speed not implemented as advertised. The marketing created consumer confusion about actual performance characteristics, as purchasers might expect performance closer to 7200 rpm based on the advertising language.

This type of technical truth that creates consumer misunderstanding represents an ethical failure. While the literal statement was accurate, the impression created was false. Consumers rely on marketing claims to make purchasing decisions, and exploiting the gap between literal truth and consumer understanding violates the spirit of fair dealing. The fact that this occurred in Western Digital's HDD business rather than flash storage is less relevant than what it reveals about corporate culture and approach to customer communication.

The WD Red drives SMR controversy emerged in 2020 and represents a more serious disclosure failure. In May 2020, Western Digital was sued for using shingled magnetic recording (SMR) technology in WD Red NAS drives without explicit consumer disclosure. SMR technology arranges data tracks on hard disk drives so that they partially overlap, like shingles on a roof, enabling higher density. However, SMR has significant performance drawbacks compared to conventional magnetic recording (CMR). SMR drives handle random writes poorly because overwriting data requires rewriting multiple overlapping tracks. This performance characteristic makes SMR technology unsuitable for RAID array applications commonly used with NAS drives.

The ethical issue is not that Western Digital used SMR technology, which has legitimate use cases for specific workloads. The issue is that the company did not explicitly disclose which WD Red drives used SMR versus CMR technology, leaving customers unable to make informed purchasing decisions. Competitor Seagate had stated explicitly that SMR should not be used in NAS applications, but Western Digital sold SMR drives into the NAS market without adequate warning.

The market response to the controversy illustrated the customer backlash. In June 2020, WD responded by introducing "Red Plus" branding for CMR drives, while "WD Red" would primarily indicate SMR technology for lighter workloads. This response essentially acknowledged that the original product labeling had been inadequate, but only after litigation and reputational damage. The incident raises questions about whether similar disclosure issues affect WD-branded flash storage products that transferred to Sandisk Corporation.

A capacity labeling settlement in March 2006 provides additional context for Western Digital's marketing practices. Western Digital settled a class-action lawsuit over hard drive capacity labeling. Drives were labeled using decimal notation, where 1 kilobyte equals 1,000 bytes, while operating systems use binary notation, where 1 kilobyte equals 1,024 bytes. This discrepancy creates perceived capacity shortfalls when drives are formatted, as a 1 TB drive might show only 931 GB of available space in Windows Explorer. WD offered $30 refunds in the form of backup software to settle the lawsuit.

While this decimal versus binary discrepancy is an industry-wide issue rather than unique to Western Digital, the settlement suggests that the company's marketing practices leaned toward exploiting consumer confusion rather than educating customers. The ethical approach would be clear disclosure of the discrepancy in marketing materials and packaging. The fact that litigation was required to change behavior suggests a pattern of prioritizing short-term sales over long-term customer trust.

These marketing controversies, spanning different Western Digital product lines and multiple years, suggest a corporate culture that tolerates technically accurate but misleading marketing. When Sandisk Corporation emerged as an independent company in 2025, it inherited both the SanDisk brand equity and the customer trust issues accumulated during Western Digital ownership. The question for consumers and enterprise customers is whether the independent Sandisk Corporation will maintain Western Digital's approach to marketing or return to SanDisk's pre-acquisition standards.

## Security Vulnerabilities and Data Protection Failures

Security vulnerabilities and data protection failures represent the most serious ethical controversies from the Western Digital era. These incidents involved products and services that were supposed to protect customer data but instead created security risks. The 2023 cyberattack that affected Western Digital systems and customer data illustrates the stakes.

Western Digital external hard drives with encryption software, marketed under the My Passport brand, were reported to have severe data protection faults. Security researchers found the encryption "easy to decrypt," with characterizations that it was "useless" and "riddled with security flaws." This represents an egregious ethical failure: customers purchased these drives specifically for encryption capabilities, paying a premium for security that did not actually exist.

The implications are severe. Customers relying on My Passport encryption for sensitive data, business information, or personal records were exposed to data theft, surveillance, or extortion. The false sense of security created by marketing encryption capabilities was potentially more dangerous than no security at all, as customers might take risks assuming their data was protected when it was not. This type of security theater exploits customer trust and creates liability.

The 2023 Western Digital cyberattack created broader data exposure. On March 23, 2023, Western Digital experienced a breach of company systems. By April 2, 2023, WD took services offline including My Cloud to assess the intrusion. The attackers exfiltrated approximately 10 TB of data including customer information. The ransom demand was described as "minimum 8 figures," meaning at least $10 million. My Cloud service was restored after 10 days on April 13, 2023, but the data exposure was permanent.

This cyberattack raises multiple ethical questions. What customer data was exfiltrated, and how were affected customers notified? What security measures were in place before the attack, and why were they insufficient? Did Western Digital maintain appropriate cyber insurance to compensate affected customers? Was the ransom paid, and if so, what were the ethical implications of funding criminal activity? These questions go beyond technical security failures to fundamental corporate responsibility.

The My Book Live data deletion incident in June 2021 added another dimension to Western Digital's security problems. Users reported that discontinued My Book Live NAS drives, last manufactured in 2013, were remotely wiped. WD advised users to disconnect these devices from the internet, highlighting security vulnerabilities in legacy connected storage products. The incident demonstrated how security vulnerabilities persist long after products leave active support, creating ongoing customer risk.

The ethical issue with My Book Live is not just the security vulnerability itself, but the inadequate support for legacy products. Customers who purchased WD-branded NAS drives had reasonable expectations of security updates throughout the product lifecycle. Remote wiping of customer data without consent represents a severe breach of trust. The fact that WD's response was essentially "unplug your old devices" shifted the burden of security failures to customers rather than accepting corporate responsibility.

These security incidents raise questions about whether similar vulnerabilities affect WD-branded flash storage products that transferred to Sandisk Corporation. Do SanDisk SSDs, USB drives, or memory cards have encryption or security features that share the same vulnerabilities as My Passport drives? Did the 2023 cyberattack affect Sandisk customer data or systems? The spin-off did not automatically resolve these security liabilities, and Sandisk Corporation inherited both the technical challenges and the reputational damage.

## Structural Risks: The Commodity Cycle Problem

Beyond specific controversies and security incidents, Sandisk Corporation faces structural risks inherent to the NAND flash business. These risks cannot be addressed through corporate responsibility initiatives or better ethical practices, as they are fundamental to the industry structure. The commodity cycle, with its extreme price volatility, represents the most significant of these structural risks.

NAND flash exhibits classic commodity supercycle behavior characterized by boom and bust phases. During boom phases, supply constraints drive prices up and manufacturers generate strong profitability. The 2021-2022 period represented such a boom, with severe shortages and price increases driven by data center buildouts and cryptocurrency mining demand. These strong profits create incentives for capacity expansion, as each manufacturer individually wants to capture more of the profitable market.

However, the expansion decisions made during boom phases create the conditions for the next bust. New fabrication facilities take years to come online, meaning that capacity added in response to 2021-2022 shortages might reach volume production just as 2024-2025 demand growth slows. The 2023 downturn, with prices falling 40-50% as oversupply emerged, illustrated this pattern. Industry losses followed, including Sandisk Corporation's 2025 pro forma net loss of $1.64 billion.

The structural problem is that no individual manufacturer has incentive to exercise restraint during boom phases. If Samsung and SK Hynix are expanding capacity, Western Digital cannot afford to stand still or it will lose market share. Each manufacturer maximizes utilization of existing capacity and invests in new capacity during boom phases, collectively creating oversupply. The bust that follows hurts all manufacturers, but no one can unilaterally avoid it without sacrificing long-term competitive position.

For Sandisk Corporation as an independent company, this commodity exposure creates existential risk. During the Western Digital ownership period from 2016 to 2025, HDD cash flow provided some stability during NAND downturns. The 2025 spin-off eliminated this diversification, leaving Sandisk Corporation shareholders to bear the full volatility of NAND cycles. The 2025 net loss of $1.64 billion illustrates the downside phase of this volatility.

The capital intensity of the NAND business amplifies this risk. New fabrication facilities cost $10-20 billion, requiring either massive accumulated profits during upcycles or access to capital markets during downturns. A standalone Sandisk Corporation must either retain sufficient earnings during good times to weather bad times or must convince lenders and investors to provide capital during industry downturns. Both approaches are challenging. Retaining earnings limits returns to shareholders and reduces competitiveness in talent acquisition. Accessing capital during downturns is expensive and may be denied if losses are too severe.

The long-term price trend in NAND flash adds another dimension of risk. Cost per gigabyte has declined approximately 25-35% annually, driven by technology scaling, manufacturing improvements, and competition. This price erosion is beneficial for consumers and creates demand growth, but it means manufacturers must continuously increase density and reduce costs just to maintain revenue per wafer. Standing still in NAND manufacturing is not an option, as competitors will inevitably advance and capture market share. This creates continuous pressure for R&D investment and capacity upgrades, regardless of current profitability.

The 2025 spin-off essentially recreated the risk profile that had necessitated the 2016 acquisition. SanDisk had been a pure-play NAND manufacturer exposed to commodity cycle volatility. Western Digital's acquisition was partly motivated by providing balance sheet stability. The fact that Western Digital chose to spin off the flash business nine years later suggests that the integration had not achieved the promised stability benefits. However, the spin-off did not eliminate the underlying commodity cycle risk, it merely redistributed it back to standalone Sandisk Corporation shareholders.

## Manufacturing Concentration and Single-Point-of-Failure Risk

The geographic concentration of NAND manufacturing creates another structural risk that ethical practices cannot address. All of Sandisk Corporation's NAND manufacturing occurs through joint ventures with Kioxia in Yokkaichi, Japan. This single location concentration creates single-point-of-failure risk that has materialized multiple times.

A June 2019 power outage at Kioxia factories in Yokkaichi resulted in loss of 6-15 exabytes of flash memory. An exabyte is one billion gigabytes, so this loss represented production capacity equivalent to millions of consumer SSDs or memory cards. The financial impact would have been substantial, though specific figures were not accessible in our research. The power outage illustrated how a single electrical infrastructure failure could disrupt global supply.

In February 2022, contamination issues at Yokkaichi and Kitakami factories affected 6.5 exabytes of output. Contamination in semiconductor fabrication can occur from airborne particles, chemical impurities, or process errors, rendering wafers unusable. The fact that this issue affected multiple factories suggests a systemic problem rather than isolated incident. The 6.5 exabytes of affected output again represented massive production loss.

These incidents demonstrate concentration risk. Natural disasters, power infrastructure failures, contamination events, or geopolitical disruptions affecting Yokkaichi would directly impact Sandisk Corporation's ability to supply products. Diversified manufacturing across multiple geographic locations would reduce but not eliminate this risk. However, the joint venture structure and capital intensity of NAND fabrication make geographic diversification difficult. Building a new fabrication facility in a different country would cost $10-20 billion and require complex negotiation with manufacturing partners.

The location of manufacturing in Japan also creates geopolitical exposure. Japan is politically stable and has strong rule of law, making it a relatively safe manufacturing location compared to some alternatives. However, Japan is subject to earthquakes, tsunamis, and other natural disasters. The 2011 Tohoku earthquake and tsunami disrupted multiple industries, including semiconductor manufacturing. While Yokkaichi was not directly affected in that incident, similar future events could impact the area.

Japan's relationship with neighboring countries also creates potential geopolitical risk. Tensions with China over territorial disputes and historical grievances could escalate into trade restrictions or economic sanctions. While such scenarios seem unlikely given current geopolitical conditions, the risk is non-zero. More plausibly, US-China technology tensions could affect equipment supply to Japanese fabrication facilities, though Japanese manufacturers have generally been excluded from the most restrictive export controls applied to China.

The dependence on joint venture manufacturing also limits strategic autonomy. Sandisk Corporation cannot unilaterally decide to expand manufacturing capacity or invest in next-generation technology without Kioxia's agreement. The governance mechanisms for these decisions, established through decades of partnership between SanDisk, Toshiba, and Western Digital, will determine how much independence Sandisk Corporation truly has. The manufacturing partnerships that enabled SanDisk's survival may constrain its options as a standalone company.

## Competitive Pressure and Scale Disadvantages

The NAND flash market has consolidated into a six-player oligopoly dominated by Asian manufacturers with scale advantages. As of Q3 2025, Samsung controlled 30% of the global NAND market, SK Hynix controlled 20%, Kioxia held 14%, Micron had 13%, Yangtze Memory Technologies (YMTC) captured 13%, and Western Digital held 11%. Sandisk Corporation's market position as a standalone company would be roughly comparable to Western Digital's 11% share, placing it as the smallest of the major NAND manufacturers.

Samsung's market position illustrates the scale advantages available to vertically integrated competitors. Samsung Electronics is a massive conglomerate with diversified businesses across consumer electronics, mobile devices, semiconductors, and displays. This diversification provides financial stability, cross-subsidization opportunities, and internal demand for Samsung's NAND products. Samsung's semiconductor division can sell NAND to Samsung's mobile division, creating a captive customer base that external competitors cannot access. Samsung was first to commercialize V-NAND (3D NAND) with 24 layers in 2013 and has maintained technology leadership through continuous investment.

SK Hynix's 20% market share reflects scale advantages achieved through both organic growth and acquisition. The December 2019 announcement that SK Hynix would acquire Intel's NAND and SSD business for $9 billion, with the deal closing in 2022, significantly expanded SK Hynix's NAND capacity. This acquisition included Intel's NAND manufacturing facility in Dalian, China, giving SK Hynix additional production capacity and geographic diversification. The scale advantages that SK Hynix gained through this acquisition make it more difficult for smaller competitors like Sandisk Corporation to compete on cost.

Micron Technology represents the only other major US-based NAND manufacturer, giving it similar geographic and regulatory positioning to Sandisk Corporation. However, Micron's 13% market share makes it slightly larger, and Micron produces both DRAM and NAND, providing diversification that Sandisk Corporation lacks. Micron's technology development capabilities, manufacturing scale, and financial resources all exceed what Sandisk Corporation can muster as a standalone company.

Yangtze Memory Technologies (YMTC) presents a different type of competitive challenge. As a Chinese state-backed manufacturer with 13% market share, YMTC has access to capital and political support that private companies cannot match. YMTC developed its own 3D NAND architecture called Xtacking, demonstrating technical capability. However, US export controls on advanced semiconductor equipment to China limit YMTC's ability to scale beyond certain technology nodes. These export controls create geopolitical friction in NAND supply chains and raise questions about fair competition between state-backed and private manufacturers.

Kioxia's position as Sandisk Corporation's joint venture partner adds complexity to competitive dynamics. Kioxia holds 14% market share, larger than Sandisk Corporation's approximately 11%, and competes with Sandisk in selling NAND flash products while simultaneously manufacturing flash through joint ventures that Sandisk relies on. This vertically integrated competitor-partner relationship requires careful management of market allocation, technology development, and customer relationships.

Sandisk Corporation's competitive differentiation historically came from several sources: retail brand strength in consumer channels, patent portfolio and licensing revenue, removable storage specialization in formats like SD and microSD cards, and professional product lines for photography and videography. However, some of these differentiators have weakened over time. Removable storage demand has declined as smartphones with cloud storage reduce need for memory cards. Asian competitors have improved their retail brand presence. Licensing revenue provides some offset but not enough to overcome scale disadvantages.

The fundamental question for Sandisk Corporation is whether its current market share and scale are sustainable. Can a company with 11% of the NAND market invest sufficiently in R&D and manufacturing to stay competitive with manufacturers that have 20-30% share? Can the SanDisk brand command sufficient pricing premium to offset cost disadvantages? Can the joint venture manufacturing model provide enough scale without strategic autonomy? The 2025 net loss of $1.64 billion suggests that the current position is challenging.

## Geopolitical Exposure and Export Controls

The semiconductor industry has become increasingly subject to geopolitical tensions and export controls, creating additional risk for Sandisk Corporation. US export controls on advanced semiconductor equipment to China, implemented from 2022-2024, affect competitor YMTC's ability to scale beyond certain technology nodes. While Sandisk Corporation does not manufacture in China and might initially benefit from restricting a competitor, export controls also create broader uncertainty in global supply chains.

The geopolitical dimension of NAND manufacturing extends beyond export controls to include technology transfer restrictions, investment scrutiny, and alliance-based technology initiatives. The US CHIPS and Science Act, passed in 2022, provides subsidies for domestic semiconductor manufacturing but also includes guardrails that restrict companies that accept funding from expanding advanced manufacturing in China. While Sandisk Corporation's manufacturing through Japanese joint ventures would not directly trigger these restrictions, the act illustrates the trend toward geopolitical fragmentation of semiconductor supply chains.

Japan's position in the global semiconductor ecosystem creates both opportunities and risks for Sandisk Corporation. Japan is a US ally with strong rule of law and robust intellectual property protection, making it a relatively safe manufacturing location compared to countries with higher political risk. However, Japan also faces demographic challenges, energy costs, and regulatory constraints that affect manufacturing competitiveness. The Japanese government's support for domestic semiconductor capability provides stability but also creates dependence on political priorities that may shift.

The Kioxia ownership structure adds geopolitical complexity. Bain Capital's 51.11% controlling stake means Kioxia is effectively controlled by US private equity, even though the company is Japanese and its manufacturing is in Japan. Toshiba's reduced 30.5% stake reflects the Japanese conglomerate's weakened financial position following accounting scandals and nuclear power losses. The involvement of US tech companies including Apple, Dell, Seagate, and Kingston as minority investors reflects concern about ensuring NAND supply diversity. This complex ownership sits within broader US-China technology competition and creates potential for geopolitical friction.

Sandisk Corporation's corporate positioning as a US-based company with manufacturing through Japanese joint ventures places it in a relatively favorable position compared to competitors with manufacturing in China or other geopolitically sensitive locations. However, this positioning does not eliminate risk. Natural disasters in Japan, trade tensions affecting equipment supply, or changes in Japanese government policy toward semiconductor manufacturing could all impact Sandisk Corporation's operations.

The spin-off from Western Digital also affects geopolitical positioning. As an independent company, Sandisk Corporation may have more flexibility to participate in alliance-based initiatives and government-supported programs. However, the company also loses the diversification that Western Digital's global operations provided. The geopolitical exposure becomes more concentrated in NAND manufacturing and markets rather than spread across HDD and flash businesses.

## Environmental Impact and Semiconductor Manufacturing

Semiconductor manufacturing has inherent environmental impacts that create ethical considerations for any NAND flash manufacturer. While specific information on SanDisk's environmental practices was not accessible in our research, the general characteristics of NAND fabrication provide context for understanding the environmental challenges.

NAND flash fabrication requires significant energy, water, and chemicals. Cleanroom facilities maintaining ISO standards for air quality and particle control consume substantial electricity. Manufacturing processes including photolithography, etching, deposition, and cleaning use various chemicals, some of which are hazardous. Water is used both for cleaning silicon wafers and for industrial cooling. The environmental footprint of a major fabrication facility is comparable to other energy-intensive industrial operations.

The manufacturing concentration in Yokkaichi, Japan, means that these environmental impacts are geographically concentrated rather than distributed. This creates local environmental burdens while potentially reducing global emissions if Japanese facilities have stricter environmental standards than facilities in other countries. The power outage and contamination incidents at Yokkaichi also illustrate how environmental and operational factors interact, with infrastructure vulnerabilities directly impacting production.

Climate change considerations add another dimension. Semiconductor manufacturing contributes to greenhouse gas emissions both directly through energy consumption and indirectly through supply chain emissions. The industry faces pressure to reduce carbon footprint, transition to renewable energy, and improve energy efficiency. These initiatives require investment that may affect profitability, particularly for mid-sized manufacturers like Sandisk Corporation with fewer resources than larger competitors.

The environmental dimension also extends to product end-of-life considerations. Flash memory devices including SSDs, USB drives, and memory cards eventually become electronic waste. The materials in these devices, including rare earth elements and potentially hazardous substances, require responsible disposal or recycling. Product design choices affect recyclability, longevity, and environmental impact. Sandisk Corporation's product design practices and end-of-life programs represent environmental stewardship considerations, though specific information was not accessible in our research.

## The 2025 Spin-Off and Risk Recreation

The February 2025 spin-off that returned Sandisk to independence also recreated the risk profile that had necessitated the 2016 Western Digital acquisition. This circular narrative raises fundamental questions about whether the NAND business can stand alone or whether it inevitably requires diversification to survive commodity cycles.

In 2016, Western Digital acquired SanDisk for $16 billion, providing balance sheet stability during NAND downturns. The acquisition rationale included diversification away from declining HDDs, access to NAND manufacturing through Toshiba joint ventures, and the potential for technology synergies. The fact that Western Digital chose to spin off the flash business nine years later suggests that these benefits did not materialize as expected. The stock market had not rewarded the combination. Cultural integration challenges had proven difficult. Strategic clarity had been sacrificed without commensurate financial benefit.

However, the spin-off did not eliminate the underlying structural risks that made the 2016 acquisition seem necessary in the first place. The commodity cycle exposure remains. The manufacturing concentration risk persists. The competitive pressure from larger Asian manufacturers continues. The geopolitical exposure through Japanese manufacturing and export controls endures. What changed was the distribution of these risks, no longer shared with Western Digital HDD investors but now borne entirely by Sandisk Corporation shareholders.

The 2025 pro forma financials illustrate the challenge. Revenue of $7.355 billion established Sandisk Corporation as a mid-tier player, but the net loss of $1.64 billion demonstrated ongoing exposure to NAND downturn phases. This loss occurred during an industry downturn that saw prices fall 40-50% from 2021-2022 peaks. Without HDD cash flow to provide stability, Sandisk Corporation must either retain earnings during upcycles or access capital markets during downturns. Both approaches are challenging for a mid-sized manufacturer.

The manufacturing paradox adds another dimension of risk. Western Digital exited flash products but still owns 50% of flash manufacturing through Kioxia joint ventures. Sandisk Corporation remains dependent on these joint ventures for supply but lacks full control over strategic decisions. The governance mechanisms for joint venture management will determine how much autonomy Sandisk Corporation truly has. The manufacturing partnerships that enabled SanDisk's survival since 2000 may constrain its options as a standalone company.

The third act of SanDisk's corporate history, beginning with the 2025 spin-off, tests whether independence is viable for a mid-sized Western NAND manufacturer. The company has advantages: the SanDisk brand in retail channels, patent portfolio and licensing revenue, manufacturing scale through Kioxia joint ventures, and technical expertise accumulated over decades. However, the disadvantages are substantial: smaller scale compared to Samsung and SK Hynix, dependence on joint venture manufacturing limiting strategic autonomy, exposure to commodity cycles without diversification, and competitive pressure from state-backed YMTC and vertically integrated Asian manufacturers.

## Ethical Assessment: Strengths, Concerns, and Structural Challenges

SanDisk Corporation's ethical profile presents a mixed picture. The company's supply chain leadership, as evidenced by the 2012 Enough Project ranking, demonstrates genuine commitment to responsible sourcing. Eli Harari's National Medal of Technology and Innovation recognizes technological contributions that serve human needs. The patent portfolio reflects legitimate innovation rather than acquisition or appropriation. These elements represent genuine ethical strengths that differentiate the company from competitors.

However, the Western Digital era controversies tarnished this ethical foundation. Product marketing issues including misleading RPM claims and undisclosed SMR technology in NAS drives exploited consumer confusion. Security vulnerabilities in self-encrypting drives exposed customers to data theft risks that marketing materials did not acknowledge. The 2023 cyberattack exfiltrated 10 TB of customer data, illustrating systemic security failures. The My Book Live remote wiping incident demonstrated inadequate support for legacy products. These controversies occurred under Western Digital ownership but affected products and customer relationships that transferred to Sandisk Corporation.

The structural risks facing Sandisk Corporation cannot be addressed through corporate responsibility initiatives but represent fundamental business challenges. The commodity cycle creates extreme volatility with no individual manufacturer having incentive to exercise restraint. Manufacturing concentration in Yokkaichi creates single-point-of-failure risk that has materialized multiple times. Competitive pressure from larger Asian manufacturers with scale advantages constrains profitability and strategic options. Geopolitical exposure through Japanese manufacturing and export controls adds uncertainty.

The 2025 spin-off both returns Sandisk to independence and recreates the risk profile that necessitated the 2016 acquisition. This circular narrative suggests that the NAND business faces structural challenges that no corporate structure can fully resolve. Whether independence proves viable will depend on execution, competitive dynamics, and commodity cycle timing. However, the ethical trajectory from supply chain leadership to security controversies represents a cautionary tale about how corporate transitions can undermine carefully built reputations.

For Sandisk Corporation to rebuild on its ethical foundation, the company will need to address the security vulnerabilities inherited from Western Digital, maintain the supply chain responsibility practices established during its independent period, and navigate structural risks without compromising on standards or transparency. The third act of SanDisk's corporate history will be judged not only on financial performance but on whether the company can reclaim the ethical leadership position it once held.
