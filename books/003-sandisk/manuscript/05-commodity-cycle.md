# The commodity cycle and financial story

On November 6, 2025, Sandisk reported the first quarter of its second independent year and told investors that demand exceeded supply in every end market, with products on allocation across the board. The NAND flash market operates as a classic commodity supercycle, characterized by periods of extreme shortage followed by extended oversupply. This cycle is not an occasional disruption but the defining structural feature of the business. For SanDisk, this meant that revenue and profitability were not determined primarily by technological leadership or brand strength, but by the timing of capacity additions relative to demand growth. The company was a pure-play NAND manufacturer without other businesses to smooth volatility, making it a hostage to these cycles. The 2016 acquisition by Western Digital was premised on the logic that an HDD business could stabilize flash volatility. Nine years later, the spin-off of Sandisk Corporation with pro forma revenue of approximately $7.4 billion and a net loss of approximately $1.6 billion revealed the limitations of that logic.

## The structure of the NAND commodity cycle

The NAND flash commodity cycle follows a predictable pattern that has repeated throughout the industry's history. During boom phases, supply constraints emerge when new applications drive demand growth faster than capacity can come online. Prices rise, manufacturers report strong profitability, and capital markets reward the sector. This creates incentives for every manufacturer to expand capacity. New fabrication facilities require $10-20 billion of investment and two to three years to build and ramp. By the time new capacity comes online, demand growth has often slowed, either because the initial application has saturated or because economic conditions have changed. The industry then enters a bust phase characterized by oversupply, price collapse, and losses across the sector.

The cycle persists because each manufacturer individually has incentive to maximize utilization of expensive fabrication capacity. Even when industry participants collectively recognize that oversupply is imminent, no single company can afford to idle capacity while competitors continue producing. This creates a classic prisoner's dilemma where rational individual behavior produces irrational collective outcomes. The capital intensity of NAND fabrication amplifies this dynamic. A facility that costs $15 billion must operate at high utilization to generate acceptable returns. Idle capacity is not just lost profit, it is destruction of shareholder value.

The long-term trend in NAND flash has been a consistent decline in cost per gigabyte of approximately 25 to 35 percent annually. This deflationary trend is not a bug but a feature of the business. It reflects the combination of technology scaling (moving from planar NAND to 3D NAND, increasing layer counts, and shrinking process geometries) and manufacturing improvements. For consumers, this deflation has been extraordinary, enabling storage that would have cost thousands of dollars in the 1990s to sell for pennies per gigabyte by the 2020s. For manufacturers, it creates a constant pressure to increase density and reduce costs just to maintain revenue per wafer. Falling prices do not automatically expand demand enough to absorb new capacity. When capacity additions outpace demand growth, prices fall faster than costs, creating margin compression.

SanDisk's financial performance tracked these cycles directly. As a pure-play NAND manufacturer without diversified business lines, the company had no buffer against flash pricing volatility. When NAND prices were rising or stable, SanDisk generated strong profits and free cash flow. When prices collapsed, losses accumulated quickly. This volatility made SanDisk a challenging stock for investors to hold, even as the long-term growth trajectory for NAND flash remained positive. The company's 2016 acquisition by Western Digital was in part a response to this reality: investors were unwilling to tolerate the volatility of a standalone flash business, and combining with a more stable HDD business was seen as a way to smooth earnings.

## The early cycles: 2006-2010

The NAND flash market experienced several significant commodity cycles in the years before and during SanDisk's independence. These cycles established the pattern that would repeat throughout the company's history.

The 2006-2007 period saw a classic oversupply cycle. NAND flash manufacturers had expanded capacity aggressively to meet demand from digital cameras, USB drives, and early mobile phones. By 2006, new capacity came online just as demand growth began to moderate. Prices for NAND flash chips declined sharply, with some grades falling by more than 50 percent within months. SanDisk, which had gone public only a decade earlier and was still establishing its position in the market, faced significant margin pressure. The company's retail brand strength provided some protection, as consumers were willing to pay premium prices for SanDisk cards and USB drives, but wholesale pricing for NAND chips affected all manufacturers similarly.

The 2008-2009 financial crisis created a demand shock that exacerbated the oversupply problem. Consumer spending on electronics collapsed, and corporate IT spending froze. This reduced demand for flash storage across all segments: retail removable storage, embedded flash in devices, and enterprise applications. NAND manufacturers responded by cutting capital expenditures, delaying new fabrication facilities, and reducing production utilization. The Toshiba-SanDisk joint venture in Yokkaichi, Japan, which had become critical to SanDisk's manufacturing strategy, scaled back expansion plans.

The 2009-2010 recovery demonstrated the other side of the cycle. As global economic conditions improved and demand for electronics recovered, NAND flash supply was constrained because manufacturers had cut capacity investments during the crisis. Prices rebounded sharply. SanDisk benefited from this recovery, with revenue and margins improving as the industry moved from oversupply back toward balance. This period reinforced the lesson that timing mattered enormously: capacity added during downturns could ride the recovery, while capacity added during peaks often became unproductive almost immediately.

## The expansion phase: 2011-2015

The period from 2011 to 2015 saw substantial growth in NAND flash demand driven by smartphones, tablets, and ultrabook PCs. Apple's iPhone, which had launched in 2007, became a massive consumer of NAND flash as volumes grew and storage capacities increased. Android smartphones followed the same trajectory. Tablets, led by the iPad from 2010, added another significant source of demand. Ultrabook PCs, promoted by Intel as thin and light laptops, increasingly used SSDs instead of hard disk drives.

This demand growth created a relatively favorable pricing environment for NAND flash through much of this period. Supply was tight enough to support healthy margins for manufacturers, but not so tight that prices spiraled upward. SanDisk's revenue grew during these years, supported by strong demand for removable storage cards (still widely used in digital cameras and as secondary storage for mobile devices) and growing penetration of embedded flash and SSDs.

However, the favorable environment also sowed the seeds of the next downturn. Strong profitability encouraged capital investment. All major NAND manufacturers announced capacity expansions. 3D NAND technology, which stacks memory cells vertically rather than trying to shrink them further on a horizontal plane, began to move from research to commercialization. This transition required significant R&D investment and new fabrication equipment. The industry was moving toward higher bit densities and lower costs per gigabyte, but the transition required capital spending that would add capacity in future years.

SanDisk navigated this period through its strategic manufacturing relationship with Toshiba. The joint venture model meant that SanDisk shared capital costs with its partner, reducing the financial burden of capacity expansion. However, it also meant that SanDisk had less direct control over capacity timing and volume. Toshiba had its own strategic considerations, including its position in other semiconductor markets and its financial commitments across multiple business lines.

The 2011-2015 period also saw SanDisk make strategic acquisitions to expand beyond its core consumer removable storage business. The acquisitions of Pliant Technology in 2011 for $327 million, SMART Storage Systems in 2013 for $307 million, and Fusion-io in 2014 for $1.1 billion were all attempts to build a presence in enterprise SSD and data center markets. These acquisitions reflected recognition that consumer removable storage would eventually face structural decline as smartphones integrated more internal storage and cloud computing reduced the need for physical media. However, the enterprise SSD market was highly competitive, dominated by Samsung and Intel, and characterized by intense price competition and rapid technological change.

## The Western Digital acquisition context: 2015-2016

The Western Digital acquisition of SanDisk, announced in October 2015 and completed in May 2016, occurred at a specific point in the NAND commodity cycle. NAND flash pricing had softened somewhat in 2015 as new capacity came online, particularly in 3D NAND. SanDisk faced the challenge of funding its share of the 3D NAND transition while navigating a more challenging pricing environment. At the same time, Western Digital's core HDD business faced long-term structural decline as SSDs replaced HDDs in PCs and began to penetrate data center applications.

From Western Digital's perspective, the acquisition served multiple strategic purposes. It provided immediate entry into NAND flash manufacturing through the Toshiba joint venture, giving WD a path to participate in the SSD market without building a NAND business from scratch. It offered diversification away from HDDs, which were clearly in structural decline. And it created potential for product synergies, such as hybrid drives that combined HDD capacity with SSD cache.

The transaction valued SanDisk at approximately $16 billion when completed in May 2016, down from the originally announced $19 billion after Chinese company Unisplendour withdrew from an agreement to acquire a 15 percent stake in Western Digital. This reduction reflected regulatory scrutiny and the complex geopolitics of cross-border technology investments.

The timing of the acquisition from a commodity cycle perspective is noteworthy. NAND flash pricing was under pressure but had not yet entered the severe downturn that would characterize 2016-2017. Western Digital was acquiring SanDisk near a cyclical peak rather than a trough, paying a premium for the flash business just as the industry was heading into a period of oversupply. This timing would prove consequential for the integration challenges and financial performance that followed.

## The post-acquisition downturn: 2016-2018

The period immediately following the Western Digital acquisition saw a significant NAND downturn. The industry investments made during 2014-2015 came online as new capacity in 2016-2017, just as demand growth moderated. Smartphones, which had driven explosive NAND demand growth, began to show signs of market saturation. Replacement cycles lengthened, and the rate of storage capacity increases per device slowed. PC shipments, which had been in decline for several years, continued to weaken.

NAND flash prices declined sharply during 2016-2018. Some product categories saw price declines of 30 to 40 percent annually during the worst of the downturn. Manufacturers reported losses or severely compressed margins. Capital spending was cut, and some manufacturers explored alternatives to the pure-play NAND model. SK Hynix's agreement to acquire Intel's NAND business, announced in October 2020 and completed in stages through early 2023, can be seen as a response to the brutal economics of standalone NAND manufacturing.

For Western Digital, the timing was unfortunate. The company had just completed a major acquisition to diversify into NAND flash, and immediately faced one of the worst NAND downturns in years. The synergy thesis for the acquisition assumed that stable HDD cash flows could support flash business volatility, but Western Digital's HDD business was itself under pressure from declining PC shipments and SSD penetration. Rather than providing stability, the combined company faced challenges in both core businesses simultaneously.

The SanDisk retail brand, which had been one of the primary assets acquired by Western Digital, faced pricing pressure during this downturn. Retail consumers became more price-sensitive as smartphone internal storage increased and cloud storage options expanded. The premium pricing that SanDisk had historically commanded became harder to sustain. Generic and lower-cost brands gained market share in retail channels.

Western Digital's financial results during 2016-2018 reflected these challenges. The company reported that revenue from its flash business segment, which included the former SanDisk operations, was under significant pressure from NAND pricing. Integration costs from the acquisition added to the financial burden. The stock market responded skeptically to the combination, with Western Digital's share price underperforming broader technology indices during much of this period.

## The recovery and COVID boom: 2019-2020

The NAND market began to recover in 2019 as manufacturers disciplined capacity additions and demand growth stabilized. The severe capital spending cuts of 2016-2018 finally took effect, reducing the rate of new capacity coming online. Supply and demand moved back into balance, and prices stabilized.

Then the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 created an unexpected and dramatic demand surge. Lockdowns and remote work requirements drove massive demand for personal computing devices. Laptop and PC shipments surged as employees and students needed equipment for work and school from home. This created strong demand for client SSDs. Data center operators, facing unexpected traffic growth from remote work, entertainment streaming, and online services, accelerated infrastructure buildouts, driving demand for enterprise SSDs.

The pandemic also disrupted supply chains and reduced manufacturing utilization at various points, creating supply constraints that further supported pricing. For a brief period in 2020, the NAND market experienced a tight supply condition that had not been seen in years. Prices firmed and in some cases increased.

Western Digital's flash business benefited from this favorable environment. Revenue from the flash segment recovered, and margins improved. The company reported that demand across consumer, client, and enterprise segments was strong. However, the durability of this demand boom was unclear. Much of the PC and client SSD demand represented pull-forward of future purchases rather than sustainable growth rates. The data center buildout was more durable but also subject to capacity planning cycles.

The COVID boom reinforced a pattern that had repeated throughout NAND history: unexpected demand shocks created temporary tight supply and profitability, which then encouraged capacity additions that eventually overshot demand. The industry's structure, with high fixed costs and pressure to utilize capacity, meant that booms inevitably led to busts.

## The severe shortage: 2021-2022

The period from 2021 through early 2022 saw a severe NAND flash shortage, one of the most pronounced in the industry's history. Multiple factors converged to create this shortage:

**Data center buildouts accelerated.** The major cloud providers, including Amazon, Google, Microsoft, and Meta, expanded infrastructure aggressively to meet growing demand for cloud services. AI training workloads began to emerge as a significant source of storage demand, with large language models requiring enormous datasets. Enterprise SSD demand grew faster than supply.

**Cryptocurrency mining created unexpected demand.** Chia, a cryptocurrency that used storage space rather than computing power for mining, launched in March 2021. This created a rush on high-capacity SSDs and HDDs as miners sought storage space for "farming." While the Chia boom proved temporary, it contributed to supply tightness during a critical period.

**Supply chain disruptions constrained output.** The pandemic continued to affect manufacturing operations, logistics, and labor availability. Power outages and contamination incidents at major fabrication facilities, including the Yokkaichi operations where Western Digital manufactured NAND through its joint venture with Kioxia, temporarily reduced output. In February 2022, contamination issues at Yokkaichi and Kitakami factories affected 6.5 exabytes of output.

**Geopolitical tensions created uncertainty.** US export controls on semiconductor equipment to China, announced in 2022, created uncertainty about future supply. While these controls primarily affected YMTC, the Chinese NAND manufacturer, they contributed to a general sense of supply insecurity that drove customers to build inventory.

NAND flash prices increased significantly during this period, with some contract prices rising 20 to 30 percent or more over the course of 2021. For the first time in years, NAND manufacturers enjoyed strong pricing power. Western Digital's flash business segment reported strong revenue growth and expanding margins. The company benefited from its manufacturing capacity through the Kioxia joint venture, which allowed it to participate fully in the upcycle.

However, the shortage also set the stage for the next downturn. Strong prices and profitability encouraged all manufacturers to accelerate capacity additions. 3D NAND layer counts increased rapidly, with manufacturers racing to add layers and increase bit density per wafer. New fabrication facilities were planned and existing facilities were expanded. The capital intensity of these investments meant that manufacturers had strong incentives to run new capacity at high utilization once it came online, regardless of immediate demand conditions.

## The 2023 downturn and price collapse

The downturn that began in 2022 and accelerated through 2023 was one of the most severe in NAND history. Prices for NAND flash declined by 40 to 50 percent or more from peak levels. The downturn was driven by multiple factors:

**Oversupply from capacity additions.** The capacity investments made during 2021-2022 began coming online just as demand growth slowed. New 3D NAND fabrication facilities increased available supply precisely when end-market demand weakened.

**PC demand collapse.** The PC boom of 2020-2021, driven by pandemic-related remote work and education needs, reversed itself in 2022-2023. Consumers and businesses that had purchased new devices during lockdowns did not need to replace them immediately. PC shipments declined sharply, reducing demand for client SSDs.

**Smartphone market saturation.** Smartphone shipments had been growing slowly even before 2023, and the economic uncertainties of that year further reduced replacement cycles. While storage capacity per device continued to increase, the overall volume of smartphones shipped declined, reducing total NAND demand.

**Data center digestion.** After aggressive capacity buildouts in 2021-2022, cloud providers took a pause to digest the infrastructure they had added. This reduced enterprise SSD demand growth.

**Inventory corrections.** During the shortage, customers had built excess inventory to ensure supply security. When supply conditions eased, these customers worked through existing inventory rather than placing new orders, magnifying the demand decline for manufacturers.

For Western Digital, the 2023 downturn was particularly damaging. The company's financial results for fiscal 2023 showed the impact of NAND pricing pressure. The flash business segment reported declining revenue despite the growth in bit shipments, meaning that the company was shipping more storage capacity but generating less revenue because of falling prices.

The pro forma financials for the 2025 spin-off illustrate the ongoing volatility. Sandisk Corporation reported pro forma revenue of approximately $7.4 billion but a net loss of approximately $1.6 billion. This loss, occurring even as revenue remained substantial, demonstrates the brutal economics of the NAND business at the bottom of the cycle. The 2025 pro forma results should not be read as an indication of permanent loss-making, but rather as evidence of the commodity cycle's continued power to swing profitability wildly from year to year.

## The HDD stabilizer logic and its failure

Western Digital's acquisition of SanDisk was premised on a specific financial logic: combining HDD and NAND flash businesses would create a more stable combined company. HDDs were seen as a mature, stable business with steady cash generation, albeit with limited growth prospects. NAND flash was seen as a growth business with higher volatility. The combination was supposed to smooth earnings and provide balance sheet strength to weather NAND downcycles.

This logic failed in practice for several reasons:

**HDD demand was less stable than expected.** The HDD market faced its own disruptions during 2016-2025. PC shipment declines reduced client HDD demand. Cloud providers increasingly used SSDs for performance-critical workloads, reducing enterprise HDD growth. While HDDs retained advantages in high-capacity bulk storage for certain applications, the market did not provide the steady cash flow that Western Digital anticipated.

**Correlated cyclical exposure.** HDD and NAND flash demand, while serving different applications, both ultimately depended on the same end markets: PC shipments, data center capex, and consumer electronics spending. Economic conditions that reduced NAND demand often reduced HDD demand simultaneously. The diversification benefit was therefore more limited than anticipated.

**Balance sheet capacity was finite.** Western Digital's ability to support the NAND business through downcycles depended on the HDD business generating sufficient cash flow. When both businesses faced pressure simultaneously, as happened during 2016-2018 and again in 2022-2023, the combined company faced significant financial strain.

**Stock market valuation never rewarded the combination.** Throughout the nine-year ownership period, Western Digital's stock market valuation never reflected the strategic logic of the merger. The company traded at a discount to pure-play HDD manufacturers and at a significant discount to SSD and NAND manufacturers. Investors simply did not accept the thesis that flash and HDD belonged together.

**Spin-off as strategic admission.** The October 2023 announcement of the planned spin-off and the February 2025 completion represented a corporate admission that the merger thesis had failed. The stated rationale for the spin-off was that HDDs and flash storage required different strategies and capital structures. This was precisely the argument that critics of the merger had made in 2015-2016.

The WD-SanDisk experience illustrates a broader truth about commodity cyclical businesses: combining two cyclical businesses in adjacent markets often creates correlation rather than diversification. When the end markets are driven by similar factors, the combined entity faces concentrated risk rather than distributed risk.

## The long-term cost curve and bit growth

Despite the volatility of the commodity cycle, the long-term trend in NAND flash has been relentlessly deflationary. The cost per gigabyte has declined by 25 to 35 percent annually for decades, driven by technology scaling and manufacturing improvements. This deflation has enabled extraordinary increases in storage capacity at the consumer level.

In the early 1990s, when SanDisk introduced its first flash-based SSD, a 20 MB drive cost approximately $1,000, or roughly $50 per megabyte. By the early 2000s, as NAND flash entered mass production in memory cards and USB drives, costs had declined to roughly $1 per megabyte. By the 2010s, costs were measured in pennies per gigabyte. By the 2020s, consumer SSDs with multiple terabytes of capacity sold for a few hundred dollars, bringing costs below $0.10 per gigabyte in many cases.

This deflationary trend was driven by multiple technological transitions:

**Planar NAND scaling.** For the first two decades of NAND flash manufacturing, the industry followed Moore's Law-like scaling, shrinking the dimensions of memory cells to fit more on each wafer. This reduced costs per bit by spreading fixed fabrication costs across more gigabytes. However, planar scaling faced physical limits as cell dimensions approached atomic scales and interference between adjacent cells increased.

**3D NAND transition.** Beginning around 2013-2014, the industry shifted from planar scaling to 3D NAND, which stacks memory cells vertically in multiple layers. Samsung was first to commercialize V-NAND with 24 layers in 2013. SanDisk and Toshiba followed with their own 3D NAND implementations. Layer counts have increased steadily, with 64-layer, 96-layer, 128-layer, 232-layer, and eventually 300+ layer generations. 3D NAND decoupled cost reduction from planar scaling, allowing continued density improvements.

**Multi-level cell technology.** NAND density improvements also came from storing more bits per cell. SLC (single-level cell) stores 1 bit per cell. MLC (multi-level cell) stores 2 bits per cell. TLC (triple-level cell) stores 3 bits per cell. QLC (quad-level cell) stores 4 bits per cell. Each increase in bits per cell dramatically increases capacity but at the cost of reduced endurance and performance. SanDisk and Toshiba introduced QLC NAND in 2009, and QLC became increasingly common in consumer applications by the 2020s.

For SanDisk and later Western Digital, the challenge was maintaining competitiveness on this cost curve while funding massive R&D and capital expenditure requirements. The Toshiba joint venture was essential to this effort, as it shared the burden of fabrication investment. However, it also created coordination challenges and reduced SanDisk's autonomy in technology roadmap decisions.

## Bit growth vs revenue growth

One of the paradoxes of the NAND flash business is that manufacturers can ship substantially more bits while generating less revenue. This occurs when price declines exceed bit growth. A manufacturer that ships 30 percent more bits year over year but faces average price declines of 40 percent will see revenue decline despite shipping more storage capacity.

This dynamic has affected SanDisk repeatedly throughout its history. The company's bit shipments have grown consistently over time as NAND flash has penetrated more applications and as storage capacities per device have increased. However, revenue has fluctuated with pricing cycles, creating periods when bit growth and revenue growth moved in opposite directions.

The 2023 fiscal year for Western Digital's flash business illustrated this paradox. The company shipped record volumes of bits, but revenue declined sharply because NAND pricing had collapsed. From a unit economics perspective, the business was growing its output and serving more customers. From a financial perspective, the business was shrinking.

This disconnect between physical output and financial results creates challenges for financial planning and investor communication. Companies must explain why growth in one metric (bits shipped) does not translate to growth in another (revenue), and why profitability can swing violently even when manufacturing operations are executing well. The complexity of this explanation often leads investors to apply valuation discounts to pure-play NAND manufacturers.

## Structural exposure and balance sheet vulnerability

SanDisk's status as a pure-play NAND manufacturer created structural balance sheet vulnerability. During NAND downcycles, when prices collapse and losses accumulate, the company faced several risks:

**Liquidity pressure.** Operating losses consume cash, and pure-play NAND companies have limited diversified revenue sources to offset these losses. During severe downturns, liquidity can become a concern, particularly if the downturn coincides with heavy capital expenditure requirements for technology transitions.

**Credit rating pressure.** Rating agencies view highly cyclical businesses with skepticism, particularly when those businesses carry substantial debt. SanDisk maintained a relatively conservative balance sheet through much of its independent history, but the acquisition by Western Digital added significant debt to the combined company.

**Strategic vulnerability.** Weak financial condition during downcycles can make a company vulnerable to acquisition or competitive pressure. SanDisk's 2016 acquisition occurred at a point when NAND pricing was weakening, and the company may have perceived limits to its ability to fund the 3D NAND transition as a standalone entity.

The 2016 Western Digital acquisition was in part a response to this structural vulnerability. SanDisk's leadership may have concluded that the balance sheet strength of a larger, more diversified company was necessary to fund ongoing participation in the NAND industry. The 2025 spin-off, returning the flash business to independence, recreated at the moment of separation the same vulnerability that existed in 2015; fiscal 2026 then inverted it, as the sections below describe.

## The pro forma 2025 financials

The pro forma financials released in connection with the 2025 spin-off provide insight into the financial state of the NAND business after nine years under Western Digital ownership:

**Revenue of approximately $7.4 billion.** This represents substantial scale, positioning the spun-off Sandisk Corporation as a mid-sized player in the NAND industry. The revenue level places the company behind Samsung, SK Hynix, and potentially Kioxia and Micron, but ahead of smaller specialists.

**Net loss of approximately $1.6 billion.** This loss, occurring in a year when the NAND market remained challenging, illustrates the continued volatility of the business. The loss should not necessarily be interpreted as evidence of permanent loss-making, but rather as a snapshot of a cyclical business at a point in the cycle. However, the magnitude of the loss raises questions about the company's ability to generate sustainable profits through a full cycle.

**11,000 employees.** The employee count indicates substantial operational infrastructure, including manufacturing, R&D, sales, and support functions. The labor cost structure of a NAND manufacturer is significant, with highly specialized engineers and technicians required for fabrication operations.

**No dividend.** The pro forma financials indicated no dividend payment, with cash flow presumably directed toward debt service, capital expenditures, and working capital needs. This contrasts with Western Digital's HDD business, which had historically paid dividends.

These financials framed the investment thesis for Sandisk Corporation post-spin-off: a cyclical business with substantial revenue and market position, but with volatility that would continue to challenge investors and management alike. The reception bore the caution out: the stock drifted to a relisting-era closing low of $29.62 by April 22, 2025 before the shortage re-rated it (Chapter 8 tells the sequence).

## The 2026 inflection: four quarters that broke the model

On November 6, 2025, Sandisk reported the first quarter of fiscal 2026 and its first solid GAAP profit as a relisted company. The quarter printed $112 million of net income on revenue of approximately $2.3 billion, above guidance of $2.1 to $2.2 billion. Management said demand exceeded supply in every end market and put products on allocation across the board, an imbalance it expected to persist through calendar 2026. The balance sheet crossed into net cash six months ahead of the target set at the spin, after a $500 million term-loan repayment. It was the mildest print of the year.

The second quarter, reported January 29, 2026, changed the scale. Revenue was approximately $3.0 billion, up 61 percent year over year, with non-GAAP gross margin of 51.1 percent against guidance of 41 to 43 percent and non-GAAP EPS of $6.20 against a guided $3.00 to $3.40. Bits grew barely at all sequentially, low single digits, because Goeckeler's team was rationing constrained supply into strengthening prices rather than chasing volume. The same report carried the Yokkaichi joint venture extension through December 31, 2034, a commitment to the manufacturing base that outlasts any single cycle.

The third quarter stopped resembling a memory company's results. Revenue was approximately $6.0 billion for the quarter ended April 3, 2026, up 251 percent year over year, at a GAAP gross margin of 78.4 percent, with non-GAAP EPS of $23.41 against guidance of $12 to $14. Goeckeler called it "a fundamental inflection point" on the April 30 call. The fourth quarter, reported August 5, 2026, went further still. Revenue was approximately $9.0 billion, up 372 percent year over year, at a GAAP gross margin of 84.6 percent, with GAAP net income of approximately $6.9 billion, or $43.97 per diluted share. About one-third of the sequential growth was volume; two-thirds was price.

The fiscal year closed at revenue of approximately $20.2 billion (on rounded quarterly prints), up 175 percent from approximately $7.4 billion in fiscal 2025. GAAP net income was approximately $11.4 billion, or $73.76 per diluted share, against a net loss of approximately $1.6 billion a year earlier. That is a swing of roughly $13 billion between two fiscal years, and the fourth quarter alone produced more revenue than the entire fiscal 2025. The year ended with approximately $4.8 billion of cash and zero funded debt. Guidance for the first quarter of fiscal 2027, given August 5, called for revenue of $10.3 to $10.8 billion and non-GAAP EPS of $44 to $46, with results due October 29, 2026.

### The price mechanism and the wafer squeeze

What broke the model was price, moving in steps the industry had never recorded. TrendForce tracked NAND contract prices up 55 to 60 percent in the first calendar quarter of 2026 and up 70 to 75 percent in the second. The third quarter rose 10 to 15 percent as consumer affordability cracked, and TrendForce forecast a further 15 to 20 percent in the fourth, with enterprise SSD the only category still accelerating. Enterprise SSD contract prices rose a cumulative 235 percent across 2026. Goldman Sachs modeled the shortage in May 2026 as the worst since 2011, with the year's NAND supply deficit at 4.2 percent of demand and the deficit persisting through 2028.

The mechanism holding supply back was arithmetic. High-bandwidth memory (HBM) earns multiples of what conventional DRAM earns on the same wafer. Its share of DRAM wafer capacity climbed from roughly 20 percent toward 30 percent during 2026, with TrendForce forecasting blended HBM prices up 121 percent in 2027. Every wafer locked into HBM was a wafer that never became ordinary DRAM or NAND, which is why the shortage deepened even after consumer demand began to crack. Memory absorbed roughly 30 percent of hyperscaler capital spending in 2026, against 8 percent in 2024, and the largest buyers kept bidding.

### Discipline first, then state money

The suppliers, burned by 2023, refused to chase the boom with private capital. Samsung cut its 2026 NAND wafer starts about 4.5 percent and SK Hynix cut about 10 percent. The discipline held even as SK Hynix printed a 76 percent operating margin in the calendar second quarter of 2026 and publicly committed to adhering to capex discipline. Then the state stepped in. Across July 2026, South Korea's government unlocked roughly $520 billion of planned fab investment from Samsung and SK Hynix under its AI semiconductor strategy, and SK Hynix committed 100 trillion won, roughly $65 billion, to a new NAND fab at Cheongju. The sequence is the cycle's next chapter in miniature: private discipline first, state-backed expansion second, and the new capacity arriving in 2028 and beyond.

### What the shortage did to everyone else

The shortage also ran over SanDisk's historical heartland, the retail shelf. Nelson Duann, a vice president at controller maker Silicon Motion, told Tom's Hardware at Computex in June 2026 that the retail SSD market had almost disappeared. Module makers' output was being redirected to PC makers who could no longer secure NAND allocation directly. Consumer DDR5 kits cost roughly 500 percent more year over year by mid-2026. VDURA's August 2026 volatility index priced a 30TB enterprise SSD at $22,600, 6.5 times its year-earlier level, and PC shipments fell more than 20 percent in the third calendar quarter of 2026 as component costs bit. Retail shelf presence, the asset SanDisk spent three decades building, became a casualty of allocation rather than demand: the customers were still there, the wafers were not.

### The New Business Model: contracts over half the bits

The strategic question inside the boom was what to do with it. Spot prices were handing Sandisk quarterly gross margins above 80 percent, and every previous upcycle had ended identically, with capacity landing into easing demand and the profit inverting into a loss. Goeckeler and CFO Luis Visoso chose to sell the shortage forward. Beginning in the third fiscal quarter of 2026, Sandisk signed multi-year New Business Model (NBM) agreements: committed quarterly volumes, minimum financial guarantees behind each contract, and pricing that mixes fixed and variable terms.

Five agreements existed by the April 30, 2026 earnings call, covering more than a third of fiscal 2027 bits. The three signed in the third quarter alone carried approximately $42 billion of minimum contractual revenue, disclosed as remaining performance obligations, a line Sandisk had never reported. Financial guarantees across the initial five exceeded $11 billion. Visoso said the agreements underwrite demand certainty "at financials consistent with our fiscal fourth quarter guidance," which meant margins near 80 percent. By August 5, 2026 the footprint had doubled: ten agreements across eight data center and edge customers, none named in disclosure, covering about half of fiscal 2027 bits and roughly two-thirds of fiscal 2028 bits. Expected gross margins run around 80 percent, with a weighted-average duration above four years. Management has sized the ten contracts at a minimum of $93.9 billion in expected revenue at floor pricing. The figure comes from management statements at the August 13, 2026 Investor Day rather than from a filing line, and it deserves that qualifier. The balance sheet corroborated the model regardless: approximately $2.5 billion of prepayments and refund liabilities at the July 2026 year end, against $25 million a year earlier.

### Returning the windfall

Capital returns followed the sequence Goeckeler had laid out: invest in the business, reach net cash, return cash. The first buyback authorization, $6 billion, was announced April 30, 2026 alongside the third-quarter report. Sandisk executed $4.5 billion of repurchases during fiscal 2026, and on August 5, 2026 the board added $14 billion, leaving $15.5 billion of authorization, on total authorizations of $20 billion, roughly the size of the entire fiscal 2026 revenue base. The arithmetic carries a tension: shares outstanding still rose 6.9 percent year over year to 146.42 million at the fiscal close, as equity compensation outran the repurchases. In the week of October 12, 2026, S&P was reported to have upgraded Sandisk to BBB from BB+ with a stable outlook. The upgrade completes the arc from a spin-off carrying roughly $2 billion of debt to investment grade with net cash, though the report is single-sourced, so the qualifier stays.

At the company's first Investor Day since relisting, August 13, 2026 in New York, management published a framework for fiscal 2028 through 2030. It promises revenue growth in the mid-to-high teens, non-GAAP gross margin sustained near 80 percent, operating margin near 75 percent, adjusted free cash flow margin near 50 percent, and a commitment to return 100 percent of excess cash to shareholders. The framework's hinge is the same hinge the contracts carry: whether margins near 80 percent survive contact with the supply response now being funded in Korea and Japan.

## Lessons from the commodity cycle experience

SanDisk's history through multiple NAND commodity cycles offers several lessons for understanding the flash memory business:

**Cycle timing is destiny.** The single most important factor in a NAND manufacturer's financial performance is often luck or skill in timing capacity additions relative to the commodity cycle. Companies that add capacity at the bottom of the cycle can ride the recovery to strong returns. Companies that add capacity at the top of the cycle face years of weak returns.

**Manufacturing scale matters.** The capital intensity of NAND fabrication creates enormous advantages for companies with scale. Larger manufacturers can spread fixed costs across more wafers, negotiate better terms with equipment suppliers, and sustain investment through downcycles. This was part of the logic for SanDisk's joint venture with Toshiba, and it remains a factor in the ongoing consolidation of the NAND industry.

**Diversification is limited by correlation.** Attempts to combine different types of storage businesses, as Western Digital did with HDDs and NAND flash, may not provide the stability benefit anticipated if the end markets are correlated by macroeconomic factors.

**Technology transitions require massive investment.** The transition from planar to 3D NAND, from SLC to MLC to TLC to QLC, and the ongoing increases in layer counts all require enormous R&D and capital expenditure. Pure-play NAND manufacturers face continuous pressure to invest or fall behind technologically.

**Geopolitical exposure is unavoidable.** NAND manufacturing is concentrated in Asia, and trade policies, export controls, and regional tensions create risks for all participants. SanDisk's dependence on manufacturing in Japan through the Toshiba joint venture created exposure to Japanese-specific risks, including natural disasters and regional economic conditions.

**The scorecard.** The spring 2026 scenarios did not survive the year: the bull marker passed inside a single fiscal year, the base band was invalidated within six months, and the bear case was falsified by the balance sheet it targeted. Chapter 10 carries the scoring.

**The dampened cycle.** The oldest lesson in this business was that no structure escapes the commodity cycle. Fiscal 2026 wrote a caveat into it: the cycle's transmission can be deliberately dampened. About half of fiscal 2027 bits and roughly two-thirds of fiscal 2028 bits sit inside multi-year contracts with minimum guarantees, floors that do not exist in a spot market. A company that spent decades as a price taker has, mid-boom, written itself a floor. What the contracts cannot do is repeal the cycle; they redistribute who absorbs it, and their variable pricing shares downside with customers by design. The structure has not been tested against the 2027 to 2028 supply response. Korea's roughly $520 billion of state-backed fab programs and SK Hynix's Cheongju NAND plant are coming, and so is the Flash Ventures partners' own joint program of more than $31 billion in Japan, announced August 27, 2026 and contingent on Japanese government support. All of it lands capacity in precisely the years the floors must hold.

The February 2025 spin-off returned Sandisk Corporation to independence as a price taker coming off a loss. Twenty months later it is a contracted supplier running an 80 percent margin framework on a floor it wrote itself. Whether that floor is load-bearing is the question the next downturn will answer, and the first reading arrives on October 29, 2026, when the company reports a quarter built substantially on the New Business Model. The floor underneath about half of SanDisk's fiscal 2027 bits has never been tested by a downturn, and the supply that would test it is already funded.
