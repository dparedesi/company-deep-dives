# Future Outlook and Competitive Position

The February 2025 spin-off that returned SanDisk to independence as Sandisk Corporation fundamentally reshapes the assessment of the 2016 Western Digital acquisition. When Western Digital completed its $16 billion acquisition of SanDisk in May 2016, the rationale was diversification: HDD stability would offset NAND volatility, creating a combined company with smoother earnings. Nine years later, the separation of the flash business implicitly acknowledged that this thesis had been flawed. The stock market had not rewarded the combination. Strategic clarity had been sacrificed without commensurate financial benefit. The question for 2026 and beyond is whether independence is more viable now than in 2016, or whether the structural challenges of the NAND business have only intensified.

Sandisk Corporation enters its third act as a mid-sized player in a six-player oligopoly. The pro forma financials for 2025 show revenue of $7.355 billion but a net loss of $1.64 billion, illustrating continued exposure to NAND commodity cycles. The company has 11,000 employees, headquarters in Milpitas, California, and relisting on NASDAQ under the original SNDK ticker. However, the competitive landscape has evolved dramatically since SanDisk first went public in 1995. Asian manufacturers dominate global production capacity. The industry has consolidated from more than a dozen players to six major manufacturers. Capital intensity has increased as 3D NAND layer counts climb beyond 200 layers. The manufacturing paradox remains: Western Digital exited flash products but still owns 50% of NAND manufacturing capacity through Kioxia joint ventures, while Sandisk Corporation remains dependent on these partnerships for supply.

The assessment of Sandisk Corporation's prospects requires addressing the fundamental strategic question raised by the spin-off. Does the separation validate the thesis that flash and HDD businesses require different strategies and capital structures? If so, why did Western Digital acquire SanDisk in the first place? Was the acquisition a failed experiment or a successful interim strategy that delivered temporary benefits before becoming untenable? The answers to these questions shape expectations for Sandisk Corporation's independent future.

## Reassessing the 2016 Acquisition: Failed Merger or Successful Interim Strategy?

The Western Digital acquisition of SanDisk completed in May 2016 at a final valuation of $16 billion, reduced from an initially announced $19 billion after Chinese company Unisplendour withdrew from acquiring a 15% stake in WD. The acquisition rationale centered on several strategic objectives: providing WD with NAND flash manufacturing capability through Toshiba joint ventures, diversifying away from declining HDDs, creating technology synergies between HDD and flash products, and achieving scale in the storage market.

The 2025 spin-off suggests that these objectives were not achieved as intended. The integration faced complications from WD's existing relationship with Toshiba. WD and Toshiba were partners in HDD manufacturing through a separate joint venture, creating complex conflicts when Toshiba sought to sell its flash business to a consortium including WD's HDD competitors. Legal disputes ensued, eventually resolved but at relationship cost. The product controversies that emerged under WD ownership, including misleading RPM claims on WD Green drives, undisclosed SMR technology in WD Red NAS drives, security vulnerabilities in self-encrypting drives, and a major 2023 cyberattack, damaged brand reputation.

From a financial perspective, the nine-year period from 2016 to 2025 likely disappointed Western Digital shareholders. Although specific total return data is not available in our research, the fact that WD chose to separate the businesses suggests that investors had not rewarded the combination. The stock market typically prefers pure-play companies with clear strategic narratives to conglomerates with unrelated businesses. WD's stock may have traded at a discount to the sum of its parts, reflecting investor preference for separate HDD and flash companies.

However, characterizing the acquisition entirely as a failure may overlook temporary benefits it provided. The WD balance sheet likely helped SanDisk navigate NAND downturns during the ownership period. The 2023 downturn, with prices falling 40-50% and Sandisk Corporation recording a $1.64 billion net loss for 2025, illustrates the type of volatility that standalone SanDisk would have faced alone. WD's HDD cash flow provided stability during this period, even if the overall combination did not create shareholder value.

R&D synergies during the WD period may have delivered benefits. The integration of SanDisk's enterprise SSD technology with WD's data center offerings created hybrid storage solutions that neither company could offer alone. Manufacturing scale through the combined Kioxia joint ventures maintained competitive position despite industry consolidation. Technology development continued through the transition from planar NAND to 3D NAND, and from early 3D generations to layer counts beyond 200.

The acquisition also prevented alternative outcomes that might have been worse. SanDisk in 2016 faced the same structural pressures that led to other industry consolidation: SK Hynix acquiring Intel's NAND business, Toshiba spinning off Kioxia, and the emergence of YMTC as a Chinese state-backed competitor. Remaining independent might have left SanDisk too small to compete effectively in capital-intensive technology development. Alternative acquirers might have been worse strategic fits than Western Digital.

The most balanced assessment may be that the acquisition delivered temporary benefits but failed as a long-term strategy. The balance sheet support during downturns, manufacturing scale maintenance, and R&D synergies provided value. However, cultural integration challenges, strategic confusion from combining different businesses, and stock market valuation that never rewarded the combination ultimately made separation the better path. The spin-off represents a corporate admission that the 2016 thesis was flawed, not necessarily that the entire nine-year period was wasted.

## Competitive Positioning in the 2026 NAND Landscape

Sandisk Corporation enters independence facing a competitive landscape that has consolidated dramatically since the original SanDisk went public in 1995. The six-player oligopoly that characterizes the NAND market as of Q3 2025 presents specific competitive dynamics: Samsung at 30% market share, SK Hynix at 20%, Kioxia at 14%, Micron at 13%, Yangtze Memory Technologies (YMTC) at 13%, and Western Digital at 11%. Sandisk Corporation's standalone position is roughly comparable to WD's 11% share, placing it as the smallest of the major NAND manufacturers.

Samsung Electronics presents the most formidable competitive challenge. As the largest NAND flash manufacturer globally with 30% market share, Samsung combines scale advantages with vertical integration across the entire technology stack. Samsung was first to commercialize V-NAND (3D NAND) with 24 layers in 2013 and has maintained technology leadership through continuous investment. Samsung's diversified businesses across consumer electronics, mobile devices, displays, and semiconductors provide financial stability and cross-subsidization opportunities. Samsung's semiconductor division can sell NAND to Samsung's mobile division, creating a captive customer base that external competitors cannot access. Samsung's technology leadership, scale advantages, and vertical integration create competitive pressures that are difficult for smaller manufacturers to match.

SK Hynix represents the second-largest NAND manufacturer at 20% market share, with scale achieved through both organic growth and strategic acquisition. The December 2019 announcement that SK Hynix would acquire Intel's NAND and SSD business for $9 billion, with the deal closing in 2022, significantly expanded SK Hynix's NAND capacity. This acquisition included Intel's NAND manufacturing facility in Dalian, China, giving SK Hynix additional production capacity and geographic diversification. SK Hynix, like Samsung, is a Korean chaebol with diversified technology businesses, providing financial stability and R&D resources. The scale advantages that SK Hynix gained through the Intel acquisition make it more difficult for smaller competitors like Sandisk Corporation to compete on cost.

Kioxia Holdings occupies a unique position as both competitor and partner. At 14% market share, Koxia is larger than Sandisk Corporation's approximately 11% share. Koxia owns what was once SanDisk's joint venture manufacturing capacity in Yokkaichi, Japan, and Western Digital (and now indirectly Sandisk Corporation) maintains manufacturing partnerships through these joint ventures. This vertically integrated competitor-partner relationship requires careful management of market allocation, technology development, and customer relationships. Koxia's IPO delays, most recently in 2023, create ongoing uncertainty about the Japanese manufacturer's ownership and strategic direction. The complex ownership structure, with Bain Capital controlling 51.11% and Toshiba holding 30.5%, adds strategic uncertainty. Koxia's larger market share and manufacturing control position give it advantages in scale and autonomy that Sandisk Corporation lacks.

Micron Technology represents the only other major US-based NAND manufacturer, giving it similar geographic and regulatory positioning to Sandisk Corporation. However, Micron's 13% market share makes it slightly larger, and Micron produces both DRAM and NAND, providing diversification that Sandisk Corporation lacks. Micron's technology development capabilities, manufacturing scale, and financial resources all exceed what Sandisk Corporation can muster as a standalone company. Micron's US-based manufacturing in Idaho and Virginia provides geographic diversification that reduces single-point-of-failure risk compared to concentration in Yokkaichi. The fact that Sanjay Mehrotra, SanDisk's former CEO, now leads Micron adds personal dimension to the competitive relationship.

Yangtze Memory Technologies (YMTC) presents a different type of competitive challenge. As a Chinese state-backed manufacturer with 13% market share, YMTC has access to capital and political support that private companies cannot match. YMTC developed its own 3D NAND architecture called Xtacking, demonstrating technical capability. However, US export controls on advanced semiconductor equipment to China, implemented from 2022-2024, limit YMTC's ability to scale beyond certain technology nodes. These export controls create both competitive opportunity and geopolitical friction. Sandisk Corporation might initially benefit from constraints on a competitor, but export controls also create broader uncertainty in global supply chains. The state-backed nature of YMTC raises questions about fair competition between companies with access to government support and those without.

Sandisk Corporation's competitive differentiation historically came from several sources. The retail brand strength in consumer channels represents a significant asset. SanDisk products were prominently featured in electronics retailers for memory cards, USB drives, and portable SSDs. This retail presence commanded premium pricing compared to generic alternatives and created consumer awareness that competitors like SK Hynix and Koxia lacked. Removable storage specialization in formats like SD and microSD cards provided market presence in segments that Asian competitors often neglected. Professional product lines for photography and videography, under the SanDisk Professional brand, served high-end customer segments with performance and reliability requirements. Patent portfolio and licensing revenue provided high-margin income that offset commodity cyclicality.

However, some of these differentiators have weakened over time. Removable storage demand has declined as smartphones with cloud storage reduce need for memory cards. The market for USB drives has softened as cloud-based file sharing and external SSDs replace thumb drives. Asian competitors have improved their retail brand presence, particularly Samsung which has strong brand recognition across consumer electronics. Licensing revenue provides some offset but not enough to overcome scale disadvantages in manufacturing and R&D.

The fundamental competitive question for Sandisk Corporation is whether current market share and scale are sustainable. Can a company with approximately 11% of the NAND market invest sufficiently in R&D and manufacturing to stay competitive with manufacturers that have 20-30% share? Can the SanDisk brand command sufficient pricing premium to offset cost disadvantages? Can the joint venture manufacturing model provide enough scale without strategic autonomy? The 2025 net loss of $1.64 billion suggests that the current position is challenging, though this loss occurred during a downturn phase that affected the entire industry.

## The Manufacturing Paradox: Strategic Autonomy versus Scale

The manufacturing paradox represents the central strategic puzzle for Sandisk Corporation. Western Digital exited flash products but still owns 50% of NAND manufacturing capacity through Western Digital-Kioxia joint ventures. Sandisk Corporation remains dependent on these joint ventures for supply but lacks full control over strategic decisions. This arrangement creates misalignment and dependency that constrain strategic autonomy.

The joint venture structure dates to May 10, 2000, when SanDisk announced a flash memory joint venture with Toshiba Corporation to form a new semiconductor company producing advanced flash memory primarily for digital cameras. This Flash Forward venture, with manufacturing facilities in Yokkaichi, Japan, became essential for competing in capital-intensive flash manufacturing. The structure survived SanDisk's acquisition by Western Digital and the spin-off of Toshiba Memory as Koxia, and now survives the Sandisk Corporation spin-off from Western Digital.

The logic of the joint venture model is straightforward. New NAND fabrication facilities cost $10-20 billion, creating barriers to entry that no standalone Western flash manufacturer could bear alone. Sharing capital costs while preserving supply capability made strategic sense for SanDisk in 2000 and continues to make sense for Sandisk Corporation in 2026. The manufacturing location in Yokkaichi became one of the world's most advanced flash fabrication sites. The joint venture model was unique among Western flash companies and explains why SanDisk survived while others exited or were acquired.

However, the dependency created by this model becomes more constraining for an independent company. Sandisk cannot unilaterally expand manufacturing capacity without Koxia's agreement. Sandisk cannot independently decide to invest in next-generation fabrication technology without partner approval. The joint venture structure that enabled SanDisk's survival since 2000 becomes a constraint on strategic autonomy as an independent company. The governance mechanisms for these decisions will determine how much independence Sandisk Corporation truly has.

The ownership structure of Koxia adds complexity to the partnership. Bain Capital controls 51.11% of Koxia, giving the private equity firm controlling interest. Toshiba Corporation retains 30.5% ownership. The remainder is distributed among other investors including Apple, Dell, Seagate, and Kingston. Western Digital owns 50% of the Western Digital-Kioxia joint ventures but does not own equity in Koxia itself. This structure creates potential misalignment: Western Digital owns half the factory but does not control the company that owns the other half.

From Western Digital's perspective, the 50% stake in NAND manufacturing provides option value without product market exposure. If NAND manufacturing becomes strategically valuable or profitable, Western Digital retains the ability to benefit. If NAND manufacturing faces oversupply and losses, Western Digital bears 50% of those losses through its joint venture stake. However, Western Digital no longer sells NAND flash products, meaning it has limited ability to influence end-market demand for its manufacturing output. This creates strategic misalignment: Western Digital owns half the factory but does not control the demand for its output.

From Sandisk Corporation's perspective, reliance on joint venture manufacturing creates supply chain dependence and strategic constraint. The manufacturing concentration in Yokkaichi creates single-point-of-failure risk. The June 2019 power outage at Koxia factories resulted in loss of 6-15 exabytes of flash memory. The February 2022 contamination issues at Yokkaichi and Kitakami factories affected 6.5 exabytes of output. These incidents demonstrate concentration risk that Sandisk Corporation cannot address through diversification.

The manufacturing paradox raises fundamental questions about long-term sustainability. Is an arrangement where one company owns 50% of manufacturing capacity but no product business, while another company has product business but must purchase supply from joint ventures it does not control, sustainable? What happens when Western Digital and Sandisk Corporation have divergent strategic interests? How are decisions about capacity expansion, technology investment, and output allocation made? The governance mechanisms established through decades of partnership will be tested as Sandisk Corporation asserts its independence.

One potential resolution would be for Sandisk Corporation to acquire Western Digital's 50% stake in the joint ventures, gaining full control of manufacturing. However, this would require capital that Sandisk Corporation may not have, particularly following a $1.64 billion net loss in 2025. Another potential resolution would be for Western Digital to acquire full control of the joint ventures, but this would make Western Digital a NAND manufacturer without a NAND product business, recreating the strategic confusion that led to the spin-off. A third possibility would be restructuring the joint ventures to give Sandisk Corporation more autonomy, but this would require agreement from Koxia and Western Digital.

The manufacturing paradox illustrates how corporate structures created in one era become constraints in another. The joint venture model that enabled SanDisk's survival as a startup in 2000 may constrain Sandisk Corporation's options as an independent company in 2026. The fact that this structure has survived multiple corporate transformations suggests both its durability and its intractability. Resolving the manufacturing paradox may be the central strategic challenge for Sandisk Corporation's third act.

## 3D NAND Evolution and Technology Leadership

The technology evolution of NAND flash represents a critical competitive dimension. The transition from planar NAND to 3D NAND, and the continuous increase in layer counts, determines cost per gigabyte, performance characteristics, and competitive positioning. Sandisk Corporation's ability to stay at or near the technology frontier will significantly influence its independent prospects.

The fundamental challenge of NAND scaling emerged in the early 2010s. Planar NAND scaling, which involved shrinking feature sizes to increase density, faced physical limits as cell-to-cell interference and leakage problems became severe. The industry transition to 3D NAND, which stacks memory cells vertically rather than shrinking them horizontally, required substantial R&D investment and new manufacturing equipment. Samsung was first to commercialize V-NAND (3D NAND) with 24 layers in 2013, gaining early technology leadership.

The evolution of 3D NAND layer counts illustrates the competitive dynamics and capital intensity of the NAND business. Early generations featured 32 layers, then 48 layers, then 64 layers. By the late 2010s, 96-layer and 128-layer NAND entered volume production. By the early 2020s, manufacturers announced 176-layer, 232-layer, and even 300+ layer technologies. Each generational increase required substantial investment in new equipment, process development, and manufacturing expertise.

Samsung maintained technology leadership through much of this evolution, leveraging its vertical integration and substantial R&D budget. SK Hynix accelerated its development through the Intel acquisition, which included advanced NAND technology and manufacturing capability. Micron Technology developed its own approach to 3D NAND with different trade-offs between density, performance, and cost. Koxia and Western Digital developed NAND through their joint ventures, sharing development costs but also constraining independent technology strategies.

Sandisk Corporation's technology position as an independent company depends on the joint venture development pathway with Koxia. The companies have jointly developed multiple generations of 3D NAND, sharing R&D costs and manufacturing capability. This collaboration has enabled staying competitive despite smaller individual scale. However, joint development also requires compromise and agreement on technology direction, potentially constraining differentiation.

The technology frontier in 2026 involves layer counts beyond 200 layers and new cell architectures. Manufacturers are exploring approaches including 4D NAND, which incorporates aspects of both 3D stacking and planar optimization, and new materials that improve endurance and performance. The capital intensity of staying at this frontier increases with each generation. New fabrication facilities for leading-edge NAND cost $10-20 billion, requiring either massive accumulated profits during upcycles or access to capital markets during downturns.

The competitive implications of technology leadership are substantial. Manufacturers at the technology frontier can produce higher density NAND at lower cost per gigabyte, creating profitability advantages. Technology leaders also gain performance advantages that matter in enterprise and data center markets where speed and reliability command premiums. Laggards must either accept lower margins on trailing-edge products or invest heavily to catch up, both of which are challenging positions.

For Sandisk Corporation, the technology question is whether the joint venture structure provides sufficient capability to stay competitive with Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron. The fact that Western Digital and Koxia have remained competitive through multiple NAND generations suggests the collaboration works. However, the spin-off changes the strategic context. Sandisk Corporation must now justify its share of R&D investment based solely on NAND product revenue, without HDD diversification. The 2025 net loss of $1.64 billion constrains financial flexibility for R&D investment.

The technology timeline creates urgency. Each generational advance typically requires 2-3 years from development to volume production. Falling behind by one generation can create multi-year competitive disadvantage. The industry is approaching practical limits on how many layers can be stacked while maintaining yield and reliability, meaning the easy gains from adding more layers may be nearing an end. Future advances may require more fundamental innovations in materials, cell architectures, or manufacturing techniques, increasing R&D risk and investment requirements.

## Emerging Technology Threats: Beyond NAND Flash

NAND flash has dominated non-volatile storage for decades, but emerging technologies pose potential threats to its long-term position. These technologies range from incremental improvements that could displace NAND in specific applications to fundamental innovations that could render NAND obsolete in certain segments. Sandisk Corporation's ability to identify and navigate these technology transitions will influence its long-term prospects.

CXL memory pooling represents one emerging trend. The Compute Express Link (CXL) standard enables high-speed connectivity between CPUs and accelerators, allowing memory to be pooled and shared across servers. This architecture creates demand for memory technologies optimized for pooling rather than traditional form factors. While NAND has roles in CXL systems for persistent memory and storage-class memory, the specific requirements of CXL applications may favor alternative technologies or require NAND optimization that creates competitive differentiation.

DNA storage represents a more radical possibility. Researchers have demonstrated data storage in synthetic DNA, achieving extraordinary density and longevity compared to electronic storage. DNA can theoretically store exabytes of data per gram, with stability measured in centuries rather than years. However, DNA storage faces substantial challenges: read and write speeds are orders of magnitude slower than electronic storage, costs are extremely high, and the technology remains experimental. DNA storage is unlikely to threaten NAND in mainstream applications within the 2026-2030 timeframe, but could address specialized archival or cold storage markets where density and longevity matter more than speed.

Phase-change memory (PCM) has been in development for decades without achieving mainstream commercial success. PCM stores data by changing the state of chalcogenide glass between amorphous and crystalline phases using heat. The technology offers advantages in speed and endurance compared to NAND, but challenges in density, power consumption, and manufacturing scalability have limited commercial adoption. Intel and Micron jointly developed 3D XPoint memory, marketed as Optane, before Intel discontinued the product in 2022. PCM may find niches in specific applications but seems unlikely to displace NAND broadly.

MRAM (magnetoresistive random-access memory) and ReRAM (resistive random-access memory) represent other emerging memory technologies. MRAM stores data using magnetic states rather than electric charge, offering advantages in speed, endurance, and power consumption. ReRAM stores data by changing resistance across a memory cell. Both technologies have found niche applications but face scaling and cost challenges for mainstream storage. These technologies are more likely to complement NAND in specialized applications than replace it broadly.

The most plausible threat to NAND comes from improvements in existing technologies rather than entirely new approaches. DRAM scaling continues to increase density, potentially reducing demand for NAND in some applications. Storage-class memory that combines DRAM-like performance with NAND-like non-volatility could displace NAND in data center applications if costs become competitive. Improvements in storage software and systems architectures, including data compression, deduplication, and tiered storage, could reduce growth in NAND demand by improving efficiency.

For Sandisk Corporation, the strategic question is how to position for technology transitions while maintaining competitiveness in current NAND markets. Several approaches are available. The company could invest in emerging technologies through internal R&D or acquisitions, building capabilities for potential transitions while maintaining NAND focus. The company could form partnerships or joint ventures with technology developers, sharing risk while maintaining option value. The company could focus on NAND market segments less vulnerable to displacement, such as embedded applications where cost and form factor matter more than raw performance. The company could monitor emerging technologies closely, positioning to move quickly when commercial viability becomes clear.

The history of storage technology transitions suggests that new technologies typically create new markets before displacing existing ones. SSDs created new markets before replacing HDDs in many applications. Flash created new markets before replacing magnetic storage in others. This pattern suggests that emerging storage technologies are more likely to create additional options rather than eliminate NAND demand entirely in the 2026-2030 timeframe. However, Sandisk Corporation must maintain technology awareness and strategic flexibility to navigate potential disruptions.

## The Independence Question: Viability in 2026 versus 2016

The fundamental question raised by Sandisk Corporation's re-emergence is whether independence is more viable now than in 2016. Several factors have changed over the nine-year period that might alter the assessment.

By 2025, this consolidation was complete, creating a more stable market structure with fewer competitive shocks. By 2025, 3D NAND was mature, and the general approach to further scaling through increased layer counts was clear. The question becomes who can execute better rather than who has the right technology approach.

The assessment of whether independence is more viable in 2026 than 2016 likely depends on perspective. From a financial stability perspective, independence is more challenging because HDD cash flow no longer provides diversification during NAND downturns. From a competitive perspective, independence is both more viable due to market consolidation and more challenging due to the scale advantages of larger competitors.

## The Third Act Verdict: Cyclical Survival or Structural Decline

Sandisk Corporation's third act, beginning with the 2025 spin-off, tests whether independence is a viable long-term strategy for a mid-sized Western NAND manufacturer. The company has genuine strengths: the SanDisk brand in retail channels, patent portfolio and licensing revenue, manufacturing scale through Koxia joint ventures, and technical expertise accumulated over decades. The 2000 joint venture model that enabled SanDisk's survival remains in place, providing manufacturing capability that the company could not independently afford.

However, the structural challenges are substantial. Smaller scale compared to Samsung and SK Hynix limits R&D investment and bargaining power with suppliers. Dependence on joint venture manufacturing constrains strategic autonomy. Exposure to commodity cycles without diversification creates financial volatility. Competitive pressure from state-backed YMTC and vertically integrated Asian manufacturers intensifies pricing pressure. The 2025 net loss of $1.64 billion illustrates the downside phase of this volatility.

The manufacturing paradox encapsulates the central dilemma. Western Digital exited flash products but still owns 50% of NAND manufacturing through Koxia joint ventures. Sandisk Corporation remains dependent on these partnerships for supply but lacks full control. The governance mechanisms for joint venture management will determine how much autonomy Sandisk Corporation truly has. The partnerships that enabled SanDisk's survival since 2000 may constrain its options as a standalone company.

The bull case scenario shows a path where independence works, but it requires favorable conditions across multiple dimensions: strong AI-driven NAND demand, effective joint venture collaboration, technology parity with competitors, and financial markets that reward pure-play NAND exposure. The base case scenario represents steady but challenged existence, surviving commodity cycles but not thriving. The bear case scenario questions whether independence is viable at all, potentially forcing strategic alternatives including sale or merger.

The historical context provides perspective. SanDisk survived as an independent company for 28 years from 1988 to 2016, demonstrating that mid-sized Western NAND manufacturers can compete. However, the industry has consolidated dramatically since that period, and the capital intensity of staying at the technology frontier has increased. The fact that Western Digital chose to separate the flash business after nine years suggests that the 2016 acquisition thesis was flawed, but this does not guarantee that independence will succeed.

Sandisk Corporation's third act will play out against the backdrop of AI transformation, geopolitical tensions, and continuous technology evolution. The company that went full circle from founding to IPO to acquisition to spin-off now faces the question of whether the circle closes with sustainable independence or whether further corporate transformations lie ahead. The answer will determine whether SanDisk's story becomes a case study in corporate resilience or another example of how structural realities of the semiconductor industry eventually overwhelm even the most determined independent companies.
